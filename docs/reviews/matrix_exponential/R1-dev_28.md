## Comprehension check

| ch | The one sentence I would say to a colleague |
|---|---|
| 01 | "e to a matrix" has a definite answer, and for the grid 0, −1; 1, 0 it is a rotation of the plane by one radian; a matrix is a rule that turns arrows into arrows, so the answer is a transformation and not a number. |
| 02 | The obvious implementation, exponentiate each entry, fails the simplest test: on the all-zeros matrix it returns four ones, which flattens the plane onto a line, where "do nothing" has to be the identity; so we need to know what e to the x really means. |
| 03 | Compounding 100% interest in finer and finer steps does not blow up, it settles at 2.71828, and in the limit the step rule becomes "your rate of growth equals your size", which is the first definition of e to the x. |
| 04 | You can build that function one term at a time, each new term chosen so its rate is the term before it, giving 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + x⁴/24 + …; taking the rate shifts the row one place and gives the same row back. |
| 05 | The tiny steps and the series reach the same number because both obey "rate equals size, start at one" and only one function can; that function solves every "rate proportional to amount" problem as e^{rt}, and it satisfies e^{a+b} = e^a e^b, which I am warned will break. |
| 06 | Make the rate i times your position and every tiny step is a sideways push, so you go round a circle; e^{it} is the point at angle t and e^{iπ} = −1. |
| 07 | The series only needs multiply, divide by a whole number and add, and square matrices can do all three, so e^A is defined by the same series; for the quarter turn J, J² = −I splits the series into cosine and sine, so e^{tJ} is the rotation by t, which is the opening answer. |
| 08 | "The matrix series converges" means every entry of the running total settles, and it always does, because the k-th term stretches by at most ‖A‖^k/k! and those bounds add up to e^{‖A‖}; e^{5J} is the worrying case whose terms climb to 26 before the factorial wins. |
| 09 | Read the matrix as a velocity field (velocity = A times position); following it for a time t moves every point by the single matrix e^{tA}, and running for s and then t is running for s + t. |
| 10 | Two stirred tanks swapping water are two linear rate equations, so one 2 by 2 matrix; one big step gives nonsense, small steps creep toward 56.77 g, e^{10A} gives it directly, and in the long run it is 50 and 50, within a gram after about 20 minutes. |
| 11 | The k-th power of a network's adjacency matrix counts walks of k steps between each pair of nodes, so e^A adds up all walks with weight 1/k!, and that number is called communicability. |
| 12 | With the Laplacian in the exponent, e^{−tL} spreads heat or chance over the network until every node has the same share, the two tanks are the two-node case, and one letter i in the exponent turns settling into sloshing for ever, which is quantum time evolution. |
| 13 | Summing the series is correct mathematics and bad arithmetic: for Moler and Van Loan's matrix the terms reach nine million and have to cancel to numbers near one, so 32-bit floats return garbage; libraries halve the matrix until it is small, approximate there (Padé), and square back up; a matrix that diagonalizes can have its eigenvalues exponentiated one at a time. |
| 14 | e^{A+B} = e^A e^B fails for matrices when AB ≠ BA: two shears in one order, the other order, or both at once give three different results, and the leading error is ½t² times the commutator AB − BA. |
| 15 | Skew matrices exponentiate to rotations; 3D has three independent spins (4D has six, one per pair of axes), the commutator of two spins is the third, and that closed set of tiny steps is a Lie algebra whose exponentials form the Lie group of rotations. |
| 16 | That is where it leads: robot poses as products of exponentials, and the Standard Model's 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 independent tiny steps matching its 12 force carriers; the matrix is the rule for tiny steps and e to the matrix is where the rule takes you. |
