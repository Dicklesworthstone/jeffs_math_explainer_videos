| 01 | They put a 2 by 2 grid where an exponent goes and say the answer is "turn the whole plane by one radian"; a matrix is a machine that takes an arrow and hands back an arrow, and the quarter-turn machine is called J. It matters because I now want to know how a grid in an exponent can come out as a turn. |
| 02 | The obvious repair, e of each entry, fails on the matrix of zeros: it gives four ones, which flatten the plane onto a line, where "e to the zero" ought to be the matrix that changes nothing. So we have to ask what e^x means. |
| 03 | Compounding 100% in finer and finer steps does not blow up; it settles at e, and in the limit the rule is "your rate of growth equals what you have", starting from one. That is the first definition of e^x. |
| 04 | You can build a function that is its own rate one piece at a time (1, x, x²/2, x³/6, x⁴/24, each piece's rate being the piece before), and only the endless sum reproduces itself. |
| 05 | The bank's limit and the series are one function, because both start at one and obey rate = amount, and only one function can; so e^{rt} solves every "changes in proportion to itself" problem, and e^{a+b} = e^a e^b. |
| 06 | If your rate is i times your position you are always pushed sideways, so you go round the unit circle, an angle t in a time t; half a turn gives e^{iπ} = −1. |
| 07 | The series only needs multiplying, dividing by whole numbers and adding, which square matrices can do, so e^A is defined by the series; J squared is −I as i squared is −1, so the series for e^{tJ} splits into cosine and sine and is the rotation by t, the opening answer at t = 1. |
| 08 | An endless sum of matrices has an answer when every entry of the running total settles, and it always does: term k is no bigger than size^k/k!, and those add up to e^{size}. Even e^{5J}, whose terms reach 26, comes home to the unit circle. |
| 09 | Read the matrix as giving every point its velocity; follow the arrows for a time t and one matrix, e^{tA}, carries every start to its finish; running for s and then t is running for s + t. |
| 10 | Two stirred tanks swapping brine obey x′ = Ax; one big step sends all the salt across, a thousand small steps give 56.75 g, e^{10A} gives 56.77 g at once, and it evens out at 50 and 50, within a gram after about twenty minutes. |
| 11 | For a network's matrix of ones and zeros, the k-th power counts k-step walks, so e^A adds up every walk with weight 1/k!; network scientists call an entry of it communicability. |
| 12 | With the Laplacian, e^{−tL} spreads heat, or the chance of finding a hopper, until it is equal on every node, hub or not; the tanks were the two-node case; put i in the exponent and it sloshes for ever instead of settling, which is quantum evolution. (I answered the quiz "the hub", and for the hopper as the card describes it I was right: R1.) |
| 13 | The series is true mathematics and a bad algorithm: on Moler and Van Loan's matrix the terms reach nine million and 32-bit arithmetic returns rubbish; halve the matrix eight times, sum a short series, square back eight times, and the same 32 bits are right to three places; libraries do that with a Padé ratio. |
| 14 | For matrices e^{A+B} = e^A e^B can fail: two shears in either order, or both at once, give three different results. It holds when AB = BA, and for short times the gap is half the commutator AB − BA. |
| 15 | Skew matrices exponentiate to rotations and give all of them; there are as many independent spins as pairs of axes, 3 in three dimensions and 6 in four; spins about different axes do not commute and their commutator is another spin. That closed system is a Lie algebra, the rotations are its Lie group, and exp is the bridge. |
| 16 | Lie groups run robot arms (a product of exponentials) and the Standard Model, whose group has 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 independent tiny steps for 12 force carriers; and the whole video is one idea: the matrix is the rule for tiny steps, e to the matrix is where the rule takes you. |
