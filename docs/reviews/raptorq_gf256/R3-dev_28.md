## Comprehension check

- **01:** You can't use retries to send a 10,000-piece file to a million lossy phones. A carousel makes the unluckiest phones wait 5–6 loops, and sending random pieces is the coupon-collector problem (about 98,000 draws). We want a scheme where any K + 2 packets are enough.
- **02:** A byte is 8 bits with place values, and XOR flips bits wherever the other operand has a 1. Doing it twice undoes it, so you can mix pieces and later unmix them.
- **03:** Each packet is an XOR equation over the pieces, so it's a row of a 0/1 matrix. The phone runs elimination and needs K independent rows, in any order. That's a fountain code, and RaptorQ (RFC 6330, 2011) is the standardised one.
- **04:** With 3 pieces the 8 possible cards are the corners of a cube. k independent cards span 2^k corners, so when you are one card short a random card is useless half the time.
- **05:** K random 0/1 cards are all independent with probability ∏(1 − 2^−i) ≈ 29%. So "exactly enough" XOR cards fail about 71% of the time, almost all of it in the last few slots, whatever K is.
- **06:** Each spare roughly halves the failure rate (about 20 spares to reach 10^−6). Some structures, like a 5-cycle, are dependent no matter how many you have. So we want coefficients from 256 values, with an arithmetic we can undo.
- **07:** u8 wrap-around multiplication can't be undone for even factors (2·128 = 0, 2·3 = 2·131 = 6). Only the low bit matters, so random wrap-around cards fail as often as XOR cards.
- **08:** Mod a prime, every nonzero number has an inverse (3·5 ≡ 1 mod 7). 256 isn't prime and 257 doesn't fit in a byte, so the 256-element field has to come from somewhere else.
- **09:** Treat a byte as a polynomial over GF(2). Adding is XOR and multiplying is carry-less, which is why 3 × 7 = 9.
- **10:** Products past x^7 must be reduced by an irreducible degree-8 polynomial. Rotation (x^8 + 1) factors and gives zero divisors (3·255 = 0). RaptorQ's x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + 1 (0x11D, low byte 0x1D) doesn't factor, so every nonzero byte has an inverse (2·142 = 1). The code is `xtime` plus shift-and-add.
- **11:** Under 0x11D, the powers of 2 visit all 255 nonzero bytes. So log/exp tables turn multiplication into adding exponents mod 255 (150·3: 180 + 25 = 205 clicks → 167), and a byte's inverse sits at 255 − its log.
- **12:** RFC 6330 multiplies with LOG/EXP lookups. In byte order the times table looks like noise; sorted by log it becomes stripes. AES's polynomial gives different products for the same bytes, so both ends must agree on the polynomial.
- **13:** With random GF(256) coefficients, the last-slot failure drops from 1/2 to 1/256 and each spare divides the risk by 256. Dense random byte cards (RLNC) need almost no overhead.
- **14:** Dense elimination costs K³/3 (4.2×10^13 steps at K = 50,000, half a day). So fast codes use sparse cards and peeling, which stalls around 98% and can never recover the ~70 pieces that are in no card.
- **15:** RaptorQ's packets are sparse XOR cards. A precode adds extra unknowns tied by fixed zero-sum equations: mostly XOR rows, plus only 10–16 GF(256) rows. The XOR cards fill all but the last H slots, and the GF(256) rows take the dangerous ones, which cuts failure from about 71% to about 0.6%.
- **16:** The decoder peels until it gets stuck, then parks (inactivates) a piece and carries it as a symbol. It solves for the parked pieces at the end, using the leftover rows and the byte rows. A byte row breaks the 5-cycle because 1 ⊕ 2 = 3 ≠ 0.
- **17:** Measured on raptorq 2.0.0 at K = 10: 0.63% failure with exactly enough packets, 24 per million with one extra, 1 per million with two (each extra divides by about 256). The standard promises 10^−2 / 10^−4 / 10^−6. The code is systematic, it ships in ATSC 3.0, and the same field (0x11D) runs Linux RAID-6 and QR codes.

## What I checked and how

- **Recomputed (Python stdlib, shift-and-add over 0x11D and 0x11B), all match:** 3·7 = 9; 2·128 = 29; 2·142 = 1; 3·255 = 0 under rotation; 0x57·0x83 = 0x31 (0xC1 under AES); 2 has order 255 under 0x11D and 51 under 0x11B; AES's 2⁻¹ = 141; EXP[0..12] = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 29, 58, 116, 232, 205; 128·4 = 58; log 150 = 180, log 3 = 25, EXP[205] = 167; 7·7 = 21 via 396 → 141 clicks; every nonzero byte's inverse sits at 255 − log; u8 log-sum wraps for 32,131 of 65,025 pairs; the 13_1 byte card evaluates to 244 with A–E = 5, 3, 7, 2, 12; the chapter 3, 14 and 16 card values; the ring (E ⊕ 2A = 6, XOR of results = 15, 3·5 = 15).
- **Probabilities and costs recomputed:** K·H_K = 97,876.06; carousel 0.368, 0.990 (per phone, loops 2 and 3) and 0.368, 0.990 (all 10^6 phones, loops 5 and 6); K = 3 success 21/64; XOR failure 71.09% (K = 10) and 71.12% (limit); 42.2%, 23.0%, 12.0% with 1–3 spares; 229,898 phones stuck; 20 spares → 9.54×10^−7 (19 → 1.91×10^−6); byte failure 0.392%, 1.53×10^−5, 5.98×10^−8; K³/3 at 50,000 = 4.17×10^13, which is 11.57 h; 14_7 coverage 73.4 of 10,000; 17_3 "≥ 1 failure" 9.15%; 6,316/24 = 263.
