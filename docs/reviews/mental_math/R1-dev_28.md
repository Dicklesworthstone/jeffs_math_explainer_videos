## Comprehension check

- **01_eighty_five_squared.** Squaring 85 the paper way in your head means keeping two rows and their carries alive at once, and the trick needs one small product (8 × 9 = 72, then 25), so the slow part was memory, never the multiplying.
- **02_ends_in_five.** A 35 by 35 square cuts into a 30 by 30 block, two 30 by 5 strips and a 5 by 5 corner; put the strips side by side and they are one strip ten wide, so the block becomes 30 by 40, which is 3 × 4 hundreds, and the corner is the 25. That is why it's "times the next number".
- **03_your_turn.** Three practice squares (25, 65, 105), then the rule in letters, (10n + 5)² = 100 × n(n + 1) + 25, and its real cost: it is only as easy as n times n + 1, so 135² needs 13 × 14.
- **04_by_parts.** Any product is a rectangle; split each side into tens and ones and you get four small products (800, 120, 120, 18 for 23 × 46), with the zeros dropped and put back by size (tens times tens are hundreds), and the adding is where it goes wrong.
- **05_running_total.** Don't store the four pieces; add each one to a running total as you get it, big piece first (800, 920, 1040, 1058), so one number is carried from step to step and every total on the way is a rough answer from below.
- **06_the_middle.** Two numbers the same distance from a middle multiply to the middle squared minus the distance squared (47 × 53 = 2500 − 9), because the strip that sticks out nearly fills the gap and misses by one small square; it only pays when the middle is easy to square.
- **07_bethe.** Feynman's story: Bethe squares 48 before the calculator does (2500, take off 200, add 4) and asks "Don't you know how to square numbers near 50?"
- **08_near_fifty.** A square near 50 differs from 2500 by two strips, each 50 by the distance, so a hundred per unit of distance, plus the small corner: put back below 50 because the two strips overlap there, new above 50. So it's 25 plus or minus the distance in hundreds, then the distance squared.
- **09_your_turn_fifty.** Practice (52, 46, 57), then the limit: the answer reads straight off as two pairs of digits only from 41 to 59; past that the small square spills into the hundreds (62² = 3700 + 144) and the formula still holds.
- **10_percents.** Percents are built from the ten percent block (move the point), half of it and double it; and x percent of y equals y percent of x because both are x times y over a hundred, which helps only when one of them is a percent you know as a fraction.
- **11_round_numbers.** Get to a round number and then correct for what you changed: times 5 is times 10 halved, halve one number and double the other (14 × 35 = 7 × 70), times 9 is times 10 less one, and $19.99 is $20 less a cent.
- **12_which_trick.** Five mixed problems where the job is to see which trick's precondition holds before computing anything.
- **13_nothing_to_grab.** 67 × 78 fits none of the tricks, so it's four pieces with carrying; use paper or a calculator, but round first (70 × 80 = 5600) so you'd notice an answer that is ten times off.
- **14_near_something_you_know.** Three of the tricks are one identity, a² = (a − d)(a + d) + d²: slide a strip and you have an easy rectangle and a small square. None of it is new (an 1844 school book prints all three), and the skill is knowing a few landmarks and looking for the nearest.
