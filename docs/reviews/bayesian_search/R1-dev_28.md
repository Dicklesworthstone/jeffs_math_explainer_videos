# R1 review — Dev, 28 — bayesian_search (Video H)

Reviewer: Dev, 28, software developer. Stage R1, the storyboard. I read the
packet (`docs/reviews/bayesian_search/R1-packet.md`) in full, in order: the
brief, the storyboard and the fact sheet. I edited nothing in the repository.

My contract: after the video I can explain the idea to a colleague and write
a toy implementation, and nothing I was told turns out to be oversimplified
in a way that bites in practice. So I wrote the toy implementation before
writing this, from the storyboard's words, and ran it against the
storyboard's numbers.

**Verdict in one paragraph.** Chapters 03 to 07 are implementable as they
stand: I wrote the map, the update and the choice rule from the words alone
and every number came out. The trouble is at the edges of that core. The
closing sentence (14_7) claims something my toy shows to be false. The dots
on screen are deleted, while the method the notes describe dims them, and
the picture cannot show the second search with whole dots. The one theorem
is stated without its reason and with one of its three conditions unspoken.
The model search over the 1968 map compares the rule with a baseline that
has no map, and its numbers have no unit of effort. Each of these has a
cheap fix, and I name it.

## Comprehension check

| chapter | what I would say to a friend |
|---|---|
| 01 | A sub is missing, and the claim to hold on to is that a search which finds nothing still tells you something: dots go out in the cell you swept and every other cell's share goes up. |
| 02 | The Scorpion sank on 22 May 1968 with all 99 men before anyone was searching, and brute force was never an option: one pass over the 12-mile patch with a 200-foot strip is about 4,600 miles of towing, and five months managed about 1,000. |
| 03 | You build the map by writing down stories of what happened, weighting them, and counting where each story's dots land; 60/40 over two stories gives 40, 30, 20, 10. |
| 04 | The Navy's map was that at scale, nine weighted stories and 10,000 simulated runs counted by cell, and the wreck lay in a cell with 85 dots next to the top cell with 1,250, so the map alone did not point at her. |
| 05 | The cell to search is the one with the biggest product of "she is there" and "I would see her", so B (30% × 2/3 = 20%) beats the likeliest cell A (40% × 1/4 = 10%). |
| 06 | An empty search of B removes the 20 dots it would have found; recounting the 80 left drops B to 12.5% and lifts every unsearched cell by 100/80, A to 50% without anyone touching it. |
| 07 | That recount is Bayes' rule: multiply each cell by the chance of an empty result if she were there, rescale to 100, pick the best p × d again (C, not A). The loop is the method. |
| 08 | Greedy on p × d is never behind any other order when the target stays put and searches cost the same; it is a 1967 theorem, and on the 1968 map a model run of it is far ahead of a row-by-row sweep. |
| 09 | A search misses because track that lands at random overlaps itself: one coverage sees 63%, two 86%, three 95%, so repeat passes give shrinking returns and never certainty (two 2-in-3 searches see 8 in 9). |
| 10 | A drifting target makes every dot move with its own wind and current, so the cloud spreads; area goes as radius squared, and the flight that sees 93% after one hour sees 13% after twelve. |
| 11 | The Coast Guard runs this loop on thousands of drifting dots; for Aldridge the first cloud was 30 miles off because the assumed time of the fall was wrong, and it was new evidence from the boat, not more empty searches, that moved it. |
| 12 | For AF 447 the map came from three weighted ingredients plus the empty searches, and because the analysts doubted the beacons they made a second map that ignored a month of silence; the wreck was in a high-chance part of that one. |
| 13 | In 1968 the at-sea books said the wreck's cell was 94% searched because the sonar was credited with an 800-foot strip it did not have; the honest figure was 65%. Overstate how well you look and the update writes off the right cell. |
| 14 | Four searches against their maps, three with a wrong input, and the lesson that the method needs honest beliefs, honest seeing estimates and an update after every empty search. The sentence I would actually say to a friend differs from 14_7: it does need the first map to leave room for the truth (D1). |

### D8: a code panel that runs

Five lines. I ran this as written (`panel.py`): after B it prints
50/12.5/25/12.5, after C 60/15/10/15, and the order of the first eight
searches is BCAABDAC.

```python
find = [p[i] * d[i] for i in range(4)]   # chance of a find, cell by cell
i = find.index(max(find))                # search the best one
p[i] *= 1 - d[i]                         # empty: that cell keeps only its misses
total = sum(p)                           # the chance the search would come back empty
p = [x / total for x in p]               # scale the map back to 100%
```

The same on dots, which is the line chapter 11 needs: a dot in the searched
cell has its weight multiplied by `1 - d`; nothing is deleted.
