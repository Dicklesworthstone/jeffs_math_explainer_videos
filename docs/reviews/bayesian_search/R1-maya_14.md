## Comprehension check

What I would say to a friend after each chapter.

- **01 the question.** A submarine is missing and they ask how you could ever find it in time, and the strange idea they tease is that searching a place and finding nothing still tells you something, because every other place gets a bit more likely.
- **02 ninety-nine.** It was real: the *Scorpion*, 1968, and all 99 men had already died before anyone knew, but the Navy still had to find the wreck, and the patch of sea was so big that passing over it once would have taken about four and a half times the towing they managed in five months, so you have to choose where to look.
- **03 a map of chances.** You make the map out of stories: two stories of what might have happened, weighted sixty to forty, turned into a hundred dots, and a cell's chance is how many dots land in it: 40, 30, 20, 10.
- **04 ten thousand dots.** The Navy did the same with nine stories and a computer dropping ten thousand dots; the top cell had one dot in every eight, the wreck was in the cell touching it, and the map did not find her by itself.
- **05 a search can miss.** The likeliest cell is not always the best one to search, because a search can miss: A has 40 but is a canyon where the wreck is seen one time in four, so B (30, seen two times in three) gives twice the chance of a find.
- **06 nothing found.** They search B and find nothing, so the twenty dots that search would have found go out, and with eighty left B drops to one in eight while A goes up to a half even though nobody searched it.
- **07 Bayes' rule.** That dot move is called Bayes' rule; you do it after every empty search and then choose again, and the next cell is C, not A, even though A now has half the chance. *(Hole in my sentence: the words of `07_2` and the letters of `07_3` went past me. I am repeating what `07_3` says aloud, not the formula.)*
- **08 where next.** Always searching the cell with the best chance of a find is proved to be the best order, no other order is ever ahead, and on the Navy's real map that plan had better than one chance in two after twenty searches when going row by row had less than one in a hundred.
- **09 the second pass.** A search misses because at sea your strips overlap and leave gaps, so one pass's worth of track covers only about 63 percent, each further pass adds less, and two searches at two in three get you to eight in nine, never to certain. *(Hole: I cannot tell you how 63 percent becomes "two times in three", or why "twice" was 86 a moment before it was eight in nine.)*
- **10 adrift.** A person in the water drifts, so the dots move and spread, and when the cloud is twice as wide the same flight covers a quarter as much, which is why the chance of spotting someone falls from 93 in 100 after an hour to 13 in 100 after twelve.
- **11 twelve hours.** The Coast Guard really does this with thousands of drifting dots; when John Aldridge fell off his boat the first map was thirty miles off because the guess about when he fell was wrong, a clue on deck fixed the story, the dots moved south, and a helicopter found him alive.
- **12 the silence.** After nearly two years of not finding the Air France plane, analysts made a dot map and had to decide whether a month of hearing no beacons meant anything, so they drew it both ways, and the wreck was in a likely part of the map that left the silence out, found within a week.
- **13 how well they looked.** In 1968 the ship's numbers said the wreck's own cell was 94 percent searched, so the map had nearly given up on it, but the sonar could not see the wreck at all, the honest number was 65, and an empty search only counts if it could have found the thing.
- **14 four maps.** Four searches side by side, three with maps that began wrong, and the point is that the method still works if you say what you believe, say honestly how well you looked and keep moving the dots; then it remembers the 99. *(Hole: I am not sure the last answer fits what I saw. Aldridge's map was put right by a clue, not by an empty search.)*
