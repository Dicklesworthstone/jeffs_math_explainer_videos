## Comprehension check

1. I'm asked how you'd find a lost sub in time, then shown a map of dots where a search that finds nothing makes the other cells brighter. I don't know why yet, but I want to.
2. In 1968 a nuclear sub with 99 men vanished; the Navy searched as if they could be saved, but they were already dead. Even finding the wreck was hopeless by brute force, because one pass over the patch would take several times everything the sled towed.
3. To decide where to look you make a map of chances: weigh two stories 60 to 40, make a hundred dots, and each cell's chance is its dot count: 40, 30, 20, 10.
4. The Navy built its map the same way (nine weighted stories, 10,000 computer runs). The wreck was in the cell next to the top cell, so the map alone did not find her.
5. The best cell to search is not the likeliest one but the one where chance-it's-there times chance-of-seeing-it is biggest, so B (20) beats A (10).
6. When a search comes back empty, the dots it would have caught go out, and every cell you didn't search goes up by a quarter, because the same one wreck now has fewer places left.
7. That move is Bayes' rule: multiply by the chance of an empty search, share out what's left, then pick the new best cell (C, not A). Search, empty, update, choose, repeat.
8. Always searching where the next search is likeliest to find it can't be beaten, because each search takes a fixed slice off the first hundred dots and slices only shrink. On the Navy's map it beats row by row, though most of the gain is just having a map.
9. A search sees a wreck only 2 times in 3 because strips laid at random leave gaps and overlaps (63% for a full cell's worth of track). A second pass catches less than the first and never makes it certain.
10. A person drifts, so the map spreads and the same helicopter track covers a smaller share every hour (93 in 100 at one hour, 13 at twelve). That is why hours matter.
11. The Coast Guard runs this on drifting dots. For John Aldridge the first map was wrong because the time he fell was guessed wrong; new evidence moved it 30 miles, and a helicopter found him alive.
12. For Air France 447 the analysts weighted three maps and, doubting what the beacons' silence meant, drew the map both ways. Both pointed at the middle, and the wreck was found within a week, in water the listening ships had crossed.
13. In 1968 the ship's numbers said the wreck's cell was 94% searched, because they believed the sonar saw 800 feet and it really saw nothing. So a map is only as good as your estimate of how well you looked.
14. The method needs a first map with room for the truth, a true sense of how well you looked, and a new map when evidence says a story was wrong. The 99 couldn't be saved, but the method now searches for people who can.

No chapter is empty or wrong for me. Chapters 08 and 13 are the two where my sentence is shakiest (M3 and M1).
