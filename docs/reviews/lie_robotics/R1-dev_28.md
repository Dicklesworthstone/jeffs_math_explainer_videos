## Comprehension check

One sentence per chapter, as I would say it to a colleague.

- **01 the book.** Two quarter turns of a book land it differently depending on the order while two slides never do, so a robot that has to hit a position and an orientation cannot treat orientation as a vector to add.
- **02 one turn.** Any two turns compose into a single turn about a single axis (two quarter turns make 120° about a diagonal), every turn can be undone, so turns form a group, and in code a turn is a 3 by 3 matrix whose columns say where the three edges point, with the later turn multiplied on the left.
- **03 three angles.** Three angles look like enough to store an orientation, but at a quarter turn of the middle ring two rings do the same job and one way of turning is gone (gimbal lock, Apollo's lamp at 70°), and every three-number scheme has such a spot somewhere.
- **04 halfway.** Averaging 170° and −170° gives 0°, the exact opposite of the real midpoint, and the fix (take the difference as a small turn on the straight line touching the circle, halve it there, roll it back) is the pattern the rest of the video repeats.
- **05 spin.** A spin is one arrow (axis times rate) that adds like any vector; holding it for one second produces an orientation, that map is the exponential and Rodrigues' formula computes it; a smooth group plus its flat piece at "doing nothing" is a Lie group and its Lie algebra.
- **06 the base.** A base that holds a constant forward-and-spin velocity drives a circle, so "pose plus velocity times time" predicts 3 m ahead when it really ends 1.91 m to the side, and the exponential is the thing that gets it right.
- **07 screws.** In space a velocity is six numbers (a twist), holding one gives a screw motion, and any change of pose at all is a single screw; a pose needs six numbers, and software stores more to avoid bad spots.
- **08 seven screws.** Each joint is a screw with no slide that moves everything beyond it, so the gripper's pose is the home pose pushed through seven exponentials from the wrist down to the base: the product of exponentials.
- **09 by hand.** With the real lengths the formula can be run by hand (elbow, then shoulder: the tip is at (0.42, 0, 0.886); joint one swings it to (0, 0.42, 0.886)), and every axis is only ever read with the arm at zero.
- **10 velocities add.** Each joint's turning gives the gripper one twist and twists add, so the seven twists side by side are the 6 by 7 Jacobian that turns joint speeds into the gripper's twist, and how hard a joint moves the tip depends on the tip's distance from that joint's axis.
- **11 lost directions.** When the arm straightens the Jacobian loses rank (4 of 6 when stretched out), and close to such a pose a modest gripper speed needs joint speeds beyond the motor's limit (160 °/s against 75 °/s at a 10° bend).
- **12 the spare joint.** Seven joints for a six-number pose leaves one over, which shows as the elbow swinging on a 29 cm circle while the gripper holds still: joint speeds whose twists cancel, the Jacobian's null space.
- **13 steps.** Inverse kinematics is a loop: read the error between the pose I have and the pose I want as one twist, ask the Jacobian which joint changes produce it, step, repeat; from this start it lands within 0.1 mm in three steps.
- **14 the straight path.** Averaging two rotation matrices leaves the set of rotations (the half-and-half blend has determinant 0.5), so the true in-between poses come from holding the connecting twist for part of a second, though planners often use a straight line for the tip and turn separately.
- **15 everywhere.** The same exponential-and-logarithm pattern runs in humanoids, drones, mapping and physics steps (adding instead of exponentiating swells a body 10% in 1,000 steps), and how much the order of two turns matters is itself a small turn, the Lie bracket.

No chapter left me without a sentence. Chapters 10 and 13 are the ones where my sentence is right and my code would be wrong (D1, D2).
