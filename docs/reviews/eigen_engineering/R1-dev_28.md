## Comprehension check

- **01:** A footbridge that passed its bounce checks swayed 7 cm sideways under walkers and was shut in two days, because it had a sideways motion of its own that no code had asked about.
- **02:** Move one of two spring-coupled carts and you must push both, so the rule from moves to pushes is a 2 by 2 table read as two sums, and no part can be solved alone.
- **03:** In exactly two directions (together, against) the pushes come back parallel to the moves, scaled by 1 and 3; those are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues, which engineers call modes.
- **04:** To go from pushes to moves, write the push as a mix of the two patterns, divide each share by its own stiffness and add back, so two coupled equations become two divisions.
- **05:** A 21-member steel truss is the same thing at size: each member is a spring EA/L added into a 21 by 21 symmetric table, one load moves all 21 directions, and one solve gives the 18 mm sag and every member force.
- **06:** That table has 21 own patterns from soft to 393 times stiffer; the softest alone gives 99.6% of the sag, but the member forces need most of the 21.
- **07:** Add mass and each pattern is an oscillator with its own frequency (5.9, 11.8, 14.9 Hz; lower when heavier), so engineers find the lowest few and ask what could drive them.
- **08:** Tacoma and the Millennium Bridge did not fail for lack of strength: each had one mode whose own motion increased the force feeding it, with a threshold of 156 to 166 walkers in London.
- **09:** A tube of air is a longer row of carts and springs whose patterns are standing waves, giving a lowest tone of c/2L (68.6 Hz for a 2.5 m bathroom).
- **10:** A hall-sized box has about two million modes below 1 kHz, each blurred by the 1.9 s decay, so above about 20 Hz they overlap and single modes stop being the useful description.
- **11:** So halls are designed with statistics and rays, single modes matter in small rooms (worst in a cube), and a real shape is cut into cells and solved like the truss.
- **12:** An economy's input table is another rule: the even mix comes back 0.7 times itself, and a table with no negative entry always has such a pattern carrying the largest eigenvalue.
- **13:** Meeting a demand means making it, plus what that uses up, and so on; the rounds shrink by 0.7 and sum to 80, or grow at 1.1, so "largest eigenvalue below one" is the whole test.
- **14:** A factory's best mix under limits looks similar but is a linear program whose answer sits at a corner (40, 20, $460); no eigenvector finds it.
- **15:** All three are tables that only multiply in their own patterns; symmetric ones always split cleanly, the economy's is rescued by having no negatives, and the idea stops at too few patterns, too many modes, or non-proportional response.
