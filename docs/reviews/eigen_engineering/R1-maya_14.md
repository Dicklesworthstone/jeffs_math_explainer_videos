## Comprehension check

- **01** A new London footbridge that had been checked for bouncing swayed 7 cm sideways under ordinary walkers and was shut in two days, because a bridge has a way of swinging that is all its own (about once a second, like a walker's side-to-side shift), so "is it strong?" is not the only question.
- **02** With two carts and three springs, moving one cart means pushing on both, so no part can be worked out alone; the pushes for any moves come out of a small table (a matrix), and moving both carts together gives pushes that look just like the moves.
- **03** Moving the carts together or exactly against each other are the only two ways where the pushes keep the same shape as the moves (once the size, or three times), and those are the carts' own patterns, the eigenvectors, with 1 and 3 as eigenvalues. (I only half followed the arrow picture: M2.)
- **04** To find where the carts go when you push one, you cut the push into half "together" and half "against", divide each half by its own number and add them back to get two thirds and one third, which matters because each pattern is one division by itself. (I needed a rewind to say why it is a division: M3.)
- **05** A real steel truss is the carts again with 21 moves and a 21 by 21 table, so ten tonnes on one joint moves every joint and a computer has to solve it all at once, getting an 18 mm sag in the middle. (Too many numbers to keep: M6, M7.)
- **06** The truss has 21 own patterns from soft to stiff, and the softest, the whole span sagging, gives 99.6% of the sag by itself, so a big structure mostly behaves like a few patterns. (I could not say whether one pattern is good enough: M9.)
- **07** Give the bridge mass and each own pattern (mode) swings at its own speed, 5.9 times a second for the slowest and slower with a heavier deck, so engineers find the slowest few and ask whether anything will push in that rhythm.
- **08** Tacoma Narrows twisted apart because the wind's push grew with the twisting, and London's bridge swayed because walkers pushed harder the more it moved, so neither was too weak: each had a mode and something feeding it.
- **09** Air in a tube is a long row of carts and springs with the same 2, −1 table, so it has own patterns too, waves that slosh in place, each with a note; a 2.5 m bathroom's lowest is 343 ÷ 5 = 68.6 hertz, which I can hum to find.
- **10** A box as big as a concert hall has nearly two million modes below 1,000 hertz, so close together that above about 20 hertz, where hearing starts, they smear into one wash.
- **11** So big halls are designed with averages and bouncing rays, single modes matter in small rooms (a cube is worst because three modes land on one note), and for a room that is not a box you cut the air into cells and do the bridge's calculation. (I could not say why the last example is a hall: M17.)
- **12** An economy uses up part of what it makes, the amounts go in a table, and making ten of each uses up seven of each, so equal amounts is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0.7: it uses up 70% and keeps 30%. (The last sentence of 12_6 went past me: M15.)
- **13** To leave people 24 of each you must also make what the making uses up, round after round, which adds to 80 because every round is 0.7 of the last; at 1.1 the rounds grow for ever, so the eigenvalue being below one says whether the economy can feed itself.
- **14** A factory choosing how many bicycles and scooters to make finds its best plan (40 and 20, $460) at a corner of a shape on a graph, with no own patterns in it, so not every question with a table is this video's kind. (I can repeat why it is in the video, but I did not feel it: M18.)
- **15** A bridge, a room and an economy are each a rule in a table, and in its own patterns a table only multiplies; that works cleanly for some tables and stops in a few places, and the footbridge got dampers once its engineers asked how it wants to move as well as how strong it is. (I could not explain "symmetric", "shear" or "exponential": M19.)
