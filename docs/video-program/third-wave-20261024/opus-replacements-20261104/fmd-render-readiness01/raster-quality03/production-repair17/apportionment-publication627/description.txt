Three towns share fifteen council seats. Add one more seat, without changing anyone's population, and the smallest town loses a seat. How can that happen?

We build Hamilton's rule from whole seats and leftover fractions, then follow those fractions around moving dials to see exactly why the Alabama paradox occurs. From there, we explore divisor rules, the quota method, population changes, and a five-town example that exposes a fundamental conflict between two promises of fairness.

Along the way, you can predict the next seat, watch the competing rules disagree, and see why there is more to fair representation than rounding carefully. We finish with what randomized apportionment can achieve, and the promises even a lottery cannot keep together.
