Why does a power series fail where the curve it describes is perfectly smooth? The answer lies above and below the real number line.

We follow one simple function, 1/(1+x²), into the complex plane. Moving grids explain multiplication and differentiation. Colour reveals zeros and poles. We prove Cauchy's theorem for squares and triangles, from shrinking squares and cancelling edges, then use it to show why boundary values determine the inside. Loops count roots without finding them, and two complex poles solve a real integral.

Along the way: polynomials, the exponential, sine, tangent, gamma and zeta; the Cauchy–Riemann equations; holomorphic functions and critical points; Cauchy's integral formula; the identity theorem; the argument principle; residues; and analytic continuation.

The limits matter too. A derivative at one point is not enough. A loop cannot pass through a zero or pole when we count its winding. Continuation can depend on the path, can meet a natural boundary, and does not make a divergent series converge.

Some calculus is helpful. We build the complex-number geometry from multiplication as a stretch and a rotation.
