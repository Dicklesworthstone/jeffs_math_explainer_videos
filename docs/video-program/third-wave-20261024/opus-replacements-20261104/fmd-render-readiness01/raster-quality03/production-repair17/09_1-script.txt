09_1	Here is the payoff. Take a function holomorphic on a disk and a little beyond its edge, and divide it by z minus a, where a is the centre. Split that quotient into two pieces. The first is f of a, times one over z minus a, and we already know its loop integral: two pi i times f of a. The second is the difference quotient: f of z minus f of a, over z minus a. It is undefined at a itself, so it has a hole in the middle, and we cannot simply call it holomorphic there. But as z approaches a, it approaches the derivative at a, so near a its size stays below some bound. Draw a small circle around the hole. Between the two circles, the difference quotient is holomorphic. Trace each circle with a polygon, and tile the ring between them with triangles, keeping the hole empty. We saw that the shrinking argument works for triangles: around each one, the integral is zero. Now add up all the triangles. Every inside edge is walked twice, in opposite directions, so those parts cancel. What survives is the outer polygon, counterclockwise, and the inner polygon, clockwise. So the two polygons, both walked counterclockwise, have the same integral. Now keep the small circle fixed, and give the polygons more and more sides, so they hug the circles. Each short chord and its arc share their endpoints, so a constant has the same integral along both. The difference comes only from how much the quotient wobbles there, and its size is at most that wobble times their combined length. Continuity makes every wobble small at once, while the total length stays bounded. So in the limit, the big circle's integral equals the small circle's, both walked counterclockwise. That holds for every small radius. Only now do we shrink the small circle. The magnitude of its integral is at most its circumference, two pi times its radius, times that bound, and that goes to zero. But the big circle's integral never changed, so it must be zero. The second piece contributes nothing, and the loop integral is two pi i times f of a.
