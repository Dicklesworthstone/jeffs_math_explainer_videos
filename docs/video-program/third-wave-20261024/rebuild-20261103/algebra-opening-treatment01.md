## The continuing object

A large square tile with four distinct corner emblems and a visibly asymmetric face. A faint receiving frame stays fixed. A rotation is an actual rigid rotation of the tile, edges and emblems together. A flip is a projected rigid turnover about a visible horizontal hinge, with a distinct reverse face; do not fake a reflection by spreading dots along arbitrary paths. Fixed corner labels belong to the frame; colored emblems belong to the tile. Never confuse these coordinate systems.

Two machines accept the same tile. One rotates then flips; the other flips then rotates. Both fit the frame, but their emblems disagree. The operation is combining actions, not adding corner numbers. The puzzle earns the abstraction: can we replace any long sequence by a single one of eight moves, undo it, and know when order can be ignored?

## Opening spoken draft and synchronized picture

1. “These two machines get the same square tile. Each machine turns it once and flips it once. Surely that should leave the same result.” Show identical large tiles; two empty step slots above each. Introduce turn and flip as separate illustrated action tokens, not unexplained letters.
2. “The left machine turns first. Watch the blue corner.” Rotate the entire left tile one quarter-turn counterclockwise, leaving a short blue arc. Hold its destination. Right tile remains unchanged.
3. “Then it flips across this horizontal hinge.” Turn over the entire left tile. Keep a ghost of the previous position only during the motion, then remove it. The blue emblem lands lower left.
4. “The right machine does the same two things in the opposite order. Before it moves, where do you think the blue corner will finish?” Introduce the right action tokens; hold with the tile still at its initial state. No answer displayed.
5. “Flip first. Then turn.” Execute the two right-hand moves separately with a settled beat between. Blue finishes upper right. Both outcomes remain visible at equal scale.
6. “Different corners. The order changed the answer. Ordinary multiplication lets us swap three times five into five times three. These actions do not work that way.” Highlight just the two blue emblems. Only now add a small crossed-out equality between the two action strips. Do not imply all numerical operations commute.
7. “But there is something remarkably tidy hiding here. Give either machine a hundred turns and flips, and the tile can finish in only eight different arrangements.” Pull back to a rack of eight visibly distinct labeled tiles. This is an assertion to prove next, not a completed proof. Do not animate a fake random hundred-step run as evidence.
8. “We will build the arithmetic of these moves. It will tell us how to shorten a sequence, how to undo it, and which details we can forget without breaking the calculation. That last question will take us all the way to quotient groups.” Return one tile to center; the eight-position rack becomes the persistent map for the next chapter. No quotient formula yet.

## Earned development, not a list of definitions

- Construct eight symmetries and prove completeness using the destination of one corner and its adjacent neighbor. The rack is generated by actual permutations.
- Compose two selected rack moves by physically applying them; mark the resulting rack slot. Build useful pieces of the multiplication table from these demonstrations before showing the whole table.
- Motivate identity and inverses by restoring a scrambled tile. Show associativity by regrouping a fixed three-action strip without reordering it; separately explain why composition is associative for arbitrary actions.
- Discover rotations as a closed subsystem; demonstrate one excluded result for a failed subset. Earn the subgroup test with a visual undo-then-do operation and then give its proof.
- Find a centralizer by testing each rack move against one chosen flip in two parallel machines. Mark the matching outcomes; distinguish the centralizer of one element from the center of the whole group.
- Use clock arithmetic when a cycle of repeated rotations motivates it. Develop modular equivalence, integer division and gcd/Euclidean algorithm as needed for cyclic subgroups; include worked proofs and counterexamples rather than just labels.
- Explain permutations as tracking destinations, cycle notation, inverse and composition, then parity with an explicit invariant and its limits. Give isomorphism as a correspondence preserving every composition, not merely equal cardinality.
- Build cosets by translating a subgroup rack. Show partition and equal-size bijections, derive Lagrange, and demonstrate why its converse fails in general with a fully verified example before stating it.
- Motivate normality by trying to multiply classes using different representatives. For rotations inside square symmetries, classes retain orientation-preserving versus reversing information; verify all representative products. Compare with a nonnormal reflection subgroup where the attempted class product fails.
- Define quotient only after that failure and repair. Connect to a homomorphism and kernel via the retained information, with a worked first-isomorphism example. Develop direct products and applications with actual computations.
