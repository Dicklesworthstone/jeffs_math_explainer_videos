Can one extra seat take a seat away?

Three regions keep exactly the same populations. Change the total number of seats and compare two rules. The seat icons show totals in each fresh allocation; they are not tracked physical chairs.

Why seats 15 and 16?

Each candidate's priority is population ÷ (current seats + 1). The largest wins; only the winner's denominator changes for the next award.

Can this example avoid both problems?

Yes. Here is one hand-picked sequence for these populations and these three house sizes. Each region's seats are within its standard quota's floor and ceiling; no region loses a seat as the house grows.

The fractional bars always show each region's standard quota: population × seats ÷ 39. Hamilton awards remaining seats by these remainders. Jefferson instead awards each next seat by population ÷ (seats already awarded + 1). All comparisons here use exact integer arithmetic; these examples have no ties for the winning priority.

This is a teaching prototype, not a historical election or a complete description of current U.S. House apportionment. Mathematical and audience review are still needed.
