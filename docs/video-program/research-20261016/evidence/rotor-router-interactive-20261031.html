Can two switches calculate a probability?

Each token starts at A and moves until it reaches an exit. At each visit, the switch sends the token along its next arrow, then flips. Compare what happens when the switches remember their positions with what happens when they are reset for every token.

Why is the target 2/3, and what is proved?

If a fair coin chooses an exit at each switch, let h(A) be the probability of reaching L first. From A, half the time the token reaches L and half the time it goes to B; from B, half the time it goes to A and half the time it reaches R. Thus h(A) = (1 + h(B))/2 and h(B) = h(A)/2, giving h(A) = 2/3.

For the remembering switches, let L and R be the number of tokens exiting left and right, x the number of A→B trips and y the number of B→A trips. Alternation gives |L − x| ≤ 1 and |R − y| ≤ 1. Conservation gives x = R + y and N = L + R, so 3L − 2N = (L − x) + (y − R). The error in the left fraction is at most 2/(3N) for N > 0.

This is a proof for this small graph, not for every routing system. The reset system does not satisfy the persistent alternation premise. The two token streams shown here are deterministic; neither is a sample of random paths.
