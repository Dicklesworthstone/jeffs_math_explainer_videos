**Proposed hook.** A field researcher has a bag of observations, some seen once and others repeatedly. Ask the viewer to predict the chance that the next independently sampled observation belongs to a category absent from the bag. Then reveal the estimate: the number of categories seen exactly once divided by the number of observations. A visual can remove each observation in turn, marking whether that observation would be new to the others; the marked fraction is the singleton fraction. The payoff would be an uncertainty-aware sequel to the fish video: chance of a new *kind*, not a count of the kinds still missing.

**Model and claim.** Draw independently from one fixed, unknown discrete distribution. The sample has size n; let F1 be the number of categories appearing exactly once, and let M_n be the sum of probabilities of all categories absent from that sample. F1/n estimates M_n; it is not an exact answer for a particular sample. McAllester and Schapire define this setting, explain the estimator and give distribution-free concentration bounds for actual missing mass: [On the Convergence Rate of Good-Turing Estimators](https://www.learningtheory.org/colt2000/papers/McAllesterSchapire.pdf), pp. 1–2, especially Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 for the bias bound. [Good's 1953 paper](https://academic.oup.com/biomet/article-abstract/40/3-4/237/195998) is the historical publication; the publisher abstract was searchable, but its full text was not accessible in this pass. The Markov-sample case needs additional analysis; do not assume independence when presenting sequential text or changing ecological populations ([Chandra, Thangaraj and Rajaraman](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01938), abstract).

**Exact argument to storyboard and independently review.** For category probabilities p_i, E[F1/n] = sum_i p_i(1-p_i)^(n-1) = E[M_(n-1)]. Meanwhile E[M_n] = sum_i p_i(1-p_i)^n, so the expected difference is sum_i p_i^2(1-p_i)^(n-1), nonnegative and at most 1/n. The leave-one-out picture explains why the estimated quantity is related to the next draw, while the n versus n−1 distinction keeps the argument honest. This is a bias statement over repeated samples, not a guarantee for the observed sample; any confidence interval shown needs its exact assumptions and bound checked.

**Small exact check.** Independently enumerating all 3^4 ordered samples with probabilities (1/2, 1/4, 1/4) gives E[F1/4] = 35/128, E[M_4] = 97/512 and difference 43/512; E[M_3] = 35/128. On the particular sample AAAA, the estimate is 0 while the true missing mass is 1/2; that sample has probability 1/16. This makes the distinction between a generally useful estimator and an exact sample-by-sample identity visible. The population is deliberately revealed to the viewer for this toy check; in the real use case it is unknown.

The exact toy enumeration can be reproduced with standard-library Python:

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

p = (F(1, 2), F(1, 4), F(1, 4))
estimate = missing = F(0)
for sample in product(range(3), repeat=4):
    chance = F(1)
    for category in sample:
        chance *= p[category]
    estimate += chance * F(sum(sample.count(i) == 1 for i in range(3)), 4)
    missing += chance * sum((p[i] for i in range(3) if i not in sample), F(0))
print(estimate, missing, estimate - missing)  # 35/128 97/512 43/512
```
