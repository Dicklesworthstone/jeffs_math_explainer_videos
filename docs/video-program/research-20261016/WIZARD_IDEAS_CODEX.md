### 1. C01 — Why turning a square can make the whole box smaller

**Hook.** Start with five identical tiles and a square tray. The viewer tries a tidy grid. A tile turns, its individual horizontal and vertical spans get larger, and nevertheless the complete arrangement fits in a smaller tray. The question is how shared geometry beats individual bounding boxes.

**Mathematical spine.** Establish area and grid bounds; build the five-square construction with exact coordinates; derive `2 + √2/2` from contacts. Then give a genuine lower-bound argument for this small case, after reconstructing its cited proof. Explain the quantifiers: one checked arrangement proves an upper bound; excluding every smaller tray proves a lower bound. A numerical search stopping proves neither optimality nor uniqueness. The [five-square case record](https://jlevy.github.io/squares/cases/5.html) gives the construction, historical attribution, bound records, and a separate fixed-side rigidity result. The [maintainer's tutorial](https://jlevy.github.io/squares/tutorial.html) distinguishes these claims and has current evidence grades for larger cases. This research pass did not independently certify the recent eleven-square theorem.

**Animation insight.** Keep all five tiles visible, show exact clearance signs at contacts, and let a putative move light up the collision it causes. Follow a numerical near-miss with an exaggerated overlap, then an exact check. This makes “fits,” “cannot be improved locally,” and “globally best” visually different statements. A brief recent-results coda can link the owner's suggested work without trying to explain an entire large machine-assisted proof.

**Audience fit.** The opening needs only squares and area; the payoff rewards visual-proof viewers with a serious optimization distinction programmers also need. A mathematician should receive exact contact geometry and the complete stated small-case theorem, not a claim that the animation itself proves it.

**Differentiation and uncertainty.** [Deckard's packing video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uL5wuiy34rs) is a substantial existing competitor; its own description emphasizes the aesthetic patterns and proposes a later mathematical treatment. [EddAardvark's seventeen-square search](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MGHNIj6qOXA) covers numerical exploration. Our proposed difference is explaining and proving one small result, then using it to teach what proof adds to search. I inspected searchable descriptions, not their complete audiovisual treatments. There may be stronger close competitors.

**Scope and rough effort.** 13–17 minutes, roughly 10–12 chapters; primarily 2D retained geometry. Estimate 4–7 focused person-days plus host render time: 1–2 for reconstructing proof and exact oracle, 1 for storyboard/script, 2–3 for animation/QA, and narration/review. These are planning guesses, not measured durations.

**Kill criterion.** Before production, a curious reader must follow a two-page proof storyboard of the chosen small theorem without hidden case analysis. ### 2. C02 — Two bad echoes can make one perfect measurement

**Hook.** Two targets produce overlapping echoes. A single long coded pulse has attractive energy but creates misleading peaks. A second code appears no better. Add their matched-filter outputs and every unwanted sidelobe vanishes in the ideal model.

**Mathematical spine.** Define correlation by sliding four signed blocks, not by opening with a Fourier transform. For `A=(1,1,1,-1)` and `B=(1,1,-1,1)`, the nonnegative-lag autocorrelations are `(4,1,0,-1)` and `(4,-1,0,1)`. Their sum is `(8,0,0,0)`. Derive the doubling construction `(A,B)` and `(A,-B)` with concatenation: opposite cross terms cancel and inherited autocorrelations add. Then derive why matched filtering a common linear response recovers `2N` times that response after the two outputs are added. The [authors' complementary-waveform chapter](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~yuejiec/papers/Golaybkch_final.pdf), §2, gives the formal identity and applications; [Jedwab's research page](https://www.sfu.ca/~jed/Research/golay.html) states the general definition and construction/classification context. The original [Golay 1961 DOI landing](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1057620) was bot-blocked here; original-history wording remains to be checked against readable primary text.

**Animation insight.** Every product in the sliding correlation has an on-screen block; pair the red and blue sidelobes so cancellation is visible before the formula. Use the same colors in the polynomial identity. An authored listen window can sonify the measurement, but the theorem must remain understandable with sound off.

**Audience fit.** This has the best ratio of surprising reveal to prerequisite burden among my engineering candidates. Four signs support a complete constructive theorem; programmers can implement the correlator. It continues the “apparently broken object repaired by algebra” appeal without repeating GF256.

**Differentiation and uncertainty.** Bounded searches for `Golay complementary pairs/sequences`, `pulse compression`, and YouTube surfaced specialist material but no clearly comparable general-audience video in the returned set. That is retrieval evidence, not evidence that no such video exists. [Davis's own talk outline](https://www.mathcs.richmond.edu/~jad/NATOtalk/outline2.html) shows substantial existing technical teaching. Avoid claiming “never explained before.”

**Scope and rough effort.** 12–16 minutes, 9–11 chapters; a stationary discrete echo model first, spectroscopy history only after verification. Estimate 4–6 person-days plus render time. No new simulation engine is needed for the central proof; arrays, sliding windows and computed plots should carry it.

**Kill criterion.** A first storyboard must explain both the recursion and why the *same* channel response must apply to the two measurements. If Doppler/motion or differing responses are silently assumed away, the brief fails. If the practical application still needs a black box after 90 seconds of setup, choose another concrete application or drop it. No claim that arbitrary ambient noise cancels, that one sequence is perfect alone, or that classification has been solved.

### 3. C03 — The honest experiment that keeps finding a false discovery

**Hook.** An experimenter uses an honest coin and an exact test. No numbers are altered. The experimenter checks at several predeclared times and stops when the result looks significant. How can a respectable calculation become a misleading experiment?

**Mathematical spine.** First build the exact one-sided binomial p-value by counting paths, and distinguish `P(data this extreme | null model)` from `P(null model | these data)`. Show two populations of experiments with different prior mixes of fair and biased coins to make the conditioning distinction concrete. Then compare one look at 200 tosses with checks at `20,40,…,200`, stopping at the first `p≤0.05`. An exact integer-path calculation in this research pass gives null rejection probabilities **0.0384188161** for the single look and **0.1391264188** for those ten looks. These are toy-model computed probabilities, not measured research misconduct or independent-test approximations.

For the constructive repair, start a toy betting capital at 1, multiplying by `1.5` after heads and `0.5` after tails. Under independent fair tosses its conditional expected multiplier is 1. Prove for a finite horizon that the probability of ever reaching 20 is at most `1/20`, using stopped nonnegative capital; pass to an unbounded horizon by increasing the finite horizon. This earns a precise anytime-valid guarantee. State what power and alternative misspecification cost; a betting process is not a magic substitute for study design. The [ASA's own statement](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf), principles 1–6, supports the interpretation boundaries. [Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe and Sekhon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08240) supply the modern time-uniform-inference bridge; the proposed elementary capital proof is a deliberately narrower theorem.

**Animation insight.** Never replace the original coin tree. Highlight paths already captured by an earlier stopping boundary, preventing double counting. Then place the betting process on the same paths: the same random outcomes, different valid decision rule. The key animation explains the mathematics rather than illustrating a lecture.

**Audience fit.** The owner liked the p-value suggestion; the channel's reported strengths include statistics. The payoff is useful to scientists and people running experiments, while staying fully reconstructible. Use a coin model to avoid implying clinical or investment advice.

**Differentiation and uncertainty.** [StatQuest's p-hacking video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HDCOUXE3HMM) already teaches multiple comparisons and subtle stopping problems; the [creator's index](https://statquest.org/video-index/) documents extensive p-value coverage. We need the *complete path-counting and anytime-valid repair*, not another list of misconceptions. This is my most competition-sensitive shortlist entry. A creator-page/video-description review is not a full competitive viewing audit.

**Scope and rough effort.** 16–20 minutes, 12–14 chapters, split the basic p-value and sequential-testing arcs cleanly. Estimate 5–8 person-days: exact probability oracle and independent check, proof review, reusable tree/path visuals, and narration/comprehension review. Never title it as “p-values are useless.”

**Kill criterion.** A viewer must correctly answer both a conditional-probability question and an optional-stopping question after the paper storyboard; predeclare a pilot criterion of at least 4 of 5 fresh readers answering both with reasons. If it becomes two separate lessons crammed together, split the production brief or defer. If a close existing video already gives this precise complete arc better, select C14 or C16 instead.

### 4. C04 — How to learn a secret about a crowd without learning each person's answer

**Hook.** The survey answers contain deliberate randomness. Can we still estimate how common a sensitive behavior is—and can a “yes” really be harmless to the person who gave it?

**Mathematical spine.** Use the two-coin variant: tell the truth with probability `1/2`, otherwise answer a private fair coin. Thus `P(Y=1|X=1)=3/4`, `P(Y=1|X=0)=1/4`, and aggregate yes frequency has expectation `q=1/4+p/2`. Derive the unbiased estimator `p̂=2Ȳ−1/2` and its variance `4q(1−q)/n`. At model prevalence `p=1/5`, the variance cost relative to direct truthful sampling is exactly `91/16`, so a similar variance requires about 5.69 times the sample size. This is my exact arithmetic, not an empirical participation effect.

Then prove the likelihood-ratio bound of 3 for either output under either private bit, and explain composition when the same person's answer is queried repeatedly. Neither complete anonymity nor independence from truth is promised. [Warner's original-paper record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12261830/) establishes the historical method; the two-coin variant must not be called Warner's exact original protocol. The [original RAPPOR paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.6981) is a primary practical bridge. The [University of Washington course treatment](https://cse163.github.io/book/module-10-beyond-163/lesson-29-privacy/randomized-response.html) presents this two-coin mechanism.

**Animation insight.** A visible channel routes truth tokens into yes/no bins; hide only the individual route while preserving aggregate counts. A dial interpolates between privacy and statistical precision. Repeat one respondent's channel and show why repeated collection spends privacy rather than creating free accuracy.

**Audience fit.** A tiny inverse problem with a useful exact result, like fish but with a different object and guarantee. Programmers get a complete mechanism. Mathematicians get the estimator's assumptions and precise probability ratios.

**Differentiation and uncertainty.** [Security and Privacy Academy already has a direct explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SD7EzSkBXug), and [Google's interactive treatment](https://pair.withgoogle.com/explorables/anonymization/) is a strong visual competitor. Our proposed addition is the linked derivation of estimator, variance, and leakage under repeat collection. No claim of a vacant niche.

**Scope and rough effort.** 13–17 minutes, 10–12 chapters, 3–5 person-days plus renders. Use synthetic people and non-sensitive fictional questions in the demonstration; there is no need to collect real secrets.

**Kill criterion.** If the storyboard leaves a fresh reader believing a yes answer conveys zero information, it fails. If estimator variance and repeated-answer composition cannot be taught without turning the episode into a differential-privacy survey, narrow the theorem or defer. Personal owner interest is still unconfirmed.

### 5. C05 — Two shadows can lie—even when every number is right

**Hook.** Reveal row and column totals for a hidden pixel picture. The viewer reconstructs it successfully—and a different picture has exactly the same measurements. Which reconstruction did the instrument actually see?

**Mathematical spine.** Begin with the 2×2 diagonal/antidiagonal switch. Formulate the binary image problem as a bipartite flow: row capacities, unit pixel edges, column capacities. Integral flows give feasible binary reconstructions. Then distinguish existence from uniqueness and explain how alternating cycles/switches connect alternatives. [Batenburg's own thesis](https://homepages.cwi.nl/~kbatenbu/papers/ba_phdthesis_2006.pdf), chapter on two-projection reconstruction, defines switching components and states Ryser's characterization. This pass independently enumerated all 512 binary 3×3 images: 328 distinct row/column-sum signatures, 98 signatures with multiple images, and a maximum of six images per signature. In particular, one pixel in every row and column allows six permutation images. Those counts are enumeration results, not claims about real scanners.

A later beat adds a genuinely new measurement and asks which ambiguity it removes. Do not claim one extra projection always ensures uniqueness. Keep nonogram *run-length* clues separate from simple sums, and discrete binary images separate from full continuous CT reconstruction.

**Animation insight.** The two “shadows” remain fixed as pixels switch. Turn the same change into an alternating cycle in the bipartite graph; the abstract graph is earned by the picture. Display the whole solution set for the small puzzle instead of choosing a convenient reconstruction and calling it truth.

**Audience fit.** Strong visual-proof appeal and a practical inverse-problem lesson: perfect measurement can still be insufficient. The programmer can build the tiny flow solver; the mathematician sees assumptions and uniqueness boundaries.

**Differentiation and uncertainty.** Targeted live searches for discrete tomography, row/column sums, nonograms, and YouTube did not return a clean direct comparable video in the bounded result set. That is an incomplete competitor investigation, not novelty proof. Nonogram tutorials and CT explainers are adjacent competitors; a pre-production manual viewing audit must compare the exact ambiguity-to-flow arc.

**Scope and rough effort.** 12–16 minutes, 9–11 chapters, 4–6 person-days plus renders. Restrict the main theorem to binary matrices with two projections; flow graphics and pixel grids should fit existing kit primitives. Do not promise arbitrary 3D scanner reconstruction.

**Kill criterion.** The proof storyboard must get from the four-pixel switch to an implementable reconstruction procedure and a precise uniqueness statement. If it stops at “inverse problems are hard,” it fails. If the two-projection theorem cannot be explained within the runtime without misrepresenting contiguous run clues, replace it with C15 or C30.
