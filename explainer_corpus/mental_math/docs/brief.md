| Topic (owner's words) | "let's make a new video about cool mental math tricks that are the most useful and practical and also WHY they work and include lots of quiz questions that we then answer after giving the people some time to think about it. The centerpiece should be "squaring numbers that end in 5" where you take the number to the left of the 5, call it n, and then compute n*(n+1) and then append "25" to the end of it. Also then we can lead into how to multiple and two 2-digit numbers in your head by breaking them into parts; like 23 times 46; that turns into 20 + 3 and 40 + 6 ; then you do the various cross products, ignoring zeros and then adding them back: 20*40+ 20*6 + 40*3 + 3*6 . For 20*40, just do 2*4 = 8 and then think about how many zeros it must have to make sense. The hardest part is remembering the 4 numbers and adding them up! Then there are other good tricks, like noticing when you can do (a + b)*(a - b), numbers that are close to 50 having easy mental math trick properties (the story Richard Feynman tells about Hans Bethe being surprised he didn't know that trick at Los Alamos, a good anedote for the video to include). You can think of a few other ones too (do web research), but they need to be fairly elementary, actually applicable and useful in real life, and doable by someone who isn't a genius." |
| Working title | **Mental Math Tricks That Actually Work (and Why)** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *Every Number Is Near Something You Know*; *Why Mental Math Tricks Work*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | A product is the area of a rectangle, and every trick worth knowing is one way to cut that rectangle into easy pieces or trade it for an equal one; see the picture once and you can square numbers ending in 5, multiply two-digit numbers, square numbers near 50 and do everyday percents in your head, and you can tell when a problem has nothing to grab and paper is the right tool. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | Eighty-five squared, done in your head the way school taught it on paper: two rows of digits and three carries to hold at once, worked from the small end, so the size of the answer arrives last. Most people lose a row. The trick needs one small product: eight times nine is seventy-two, then twenty-five. The school method is not wrong; it was built for paper, and paper does the remembering. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | A quiz before anything is taught: "eighty-five squared", a four-second countdown, then the answer, 7,225, and the two steps that give it. It previews the payoff and the video's recurring device at once; then the title; then the honest failure of the paper method in chapter 01. |
| The pictures | (1) **The strip that moves.** A square of side 35 is a 30 by 30 block, two strips 30 by 5 and a 5 by 5 corner; the two strips join into one strip 10 wide, so the block grows to 30 by 40 (three times four hundreds) and the corner is the 25. The same move, a strip sliding from one side to another, is squares near 50 (two strips of 50 make one strip of 100: "that many hundreds") and (a + b)(a − b) (a rectangle one strip short of a square). The video ends on its rigorous form, a² = (a − d)(a + d) + d², which contains all three. (2) **The rectangle cut into parts**: 23 by 46 as four pieces, 20 × 40, 20 × 6, 3 × 40 and 3 × 6, added biggest first as a running total. The same rectangle, cut in half and restacked, is doubling and halving; with a sliver sliced off, it is "round, then fix"; turned on its side, it is x% of y = y% of x. Every trick in the video is one move on a rectangle. |
| Tricks, and why these | The owner's four: squares ending in 5 (the centrepiece); two-digit products by parts with a running total; (a + b)(a − b); squares near 50 with the Feynman and Bethe story. Four more from the research, in two short chapters, each judged by his three tests (elementary, useful in real life, doable by someone who isn't a genius) (default): **percents by blocks** (10%, half of it, double it: tips, tax, discounts) and **the swap**, x% of y = y% of x; **halving and doubling** (×5 is ×10 then half, 14 × 35 = 7 × 70) and **round, then fix** (×9, prices that end in .99), taught together as "get to a round number". "Estimate first" is a habit taught inside the by-parts chapter and again at the end, not a trick of its own. Left out, with the reason: **the rule of 72** (useful, but it is the one candidate whose picture is not a rectangle, and an honest why, with where the rule drifts, costs about 105 s by the storyboard estimate, which would push the video past 15 minutes; it is ready to come back as its own chapter if the owner wants it, and the research on it, down to Pacioli's 1494 page, is in the fact sheet); ×11 and products near 100 (rarely met in real life, and the same family as the core); casting out nines (no use since calculators, and it misses about one wrong answer in nine); temperature and mile conversions (rules of thumb with no picture worth a chapter). |
| The "your turn" device | A card with the problem, a visible countdown ring of 4, 5 or 6 seconds by difficulty, the music alone and the narration silent, the line "pause for more time" on the card every time (spoken at the first quiz and again before the mixed round), then the answer worked through with the trick in the same place on screen. Twenty-two quizzes: the cold open; three on squares ending in 5; two on parts; two on the middle; three near 50; two on percents; three on round numbers; a mixed round of five where the viewer must first pick which trick fits; and a last product with nothing to grab. They climb from easy to hard inside each group. Every quiz number and answer comes from `facts.rs`, pinned against `oracle.py` (default for the counts and the seconds). |
| Worked examples | 35² = 1225 (3 × 4 = 12, then 25); 85² = 7225; 105² = 11025 (10 × 11 = 110); 23 × 46 = 800 + 120 + 120 + 18 = 1058, as a running total 800, 920, 1040, 1058; 47 × 53 = 50² − 3² = 2491; 48² = 2304 and 53² = 2809 (25 plus or minus the distance for the hundreds, then the distance squared); 15% of 48 = 4.80 + 2.40 = 7.20; 8% of 25 = 25% of 8 = 2; 5 × 86 = 430; 14 × 35 = 7 × 70 = 490; 9 × 37 = 370 − 37 = 333; 4 × 19.99 = 80 − 0.04 = 79.96; 67 × 78 = 5226, the product no trick helps with (about 70 × 80 = 5600). The fact sheet has the full list with every quiz. |
| Spoken vocabulary (plan rule 21) | **trick** (never "hack", "shortcut", "secret" or "method"); **times** (never "multiplied by"); **squared**; **the number in front** (the digits left of the 5); **parts** (the tens and the ones a number breaks into) and **pieces** (the smaller rectangles); **block**, **strip** and **corner** (the three kinds of piece); **running total**; **how big** (for the zeros; never "magnitude"); **the middle** and **the distance**; **your turn** (every quiz); **round, then fix**; **double** and **halve**; **percent**; **paper** (the written method). Never "genius", "human calculator", "instantly" or "lightning". The formal names appear once each, in a lower-third: the distributive law, difference of squares, the area model. |
| Notation (screen) | Numbers before letters (plan rule 22). `n` for the digits in front of the 5 (the owner's letter), with the word label "the number in front"; `d` for the distance from 50, labelled "how far from 50"; `a` and `b` only on screen in `(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²`, spoken as "the middle" and "the distance" so the letter a is never read aloud beside the number eight; × for times, never a dot or an asterisk; money with two decimals; percents as `15%`. No Greek anywhere. |
| What the script must get right | (1) "Write 25 after it" means 100 × n(n + 1) + 25, for every whole number n from 0 up (5² = 25 is the edge: zero times one); with a decimal point it still works (7.5² = 56.25). The trick is only as easy as n times the next number (135² needs 13 × 14). (2) The zeros come back by size: tens times tens are hundreds. (3) Biggest piece first, so the first number said is already a fair estimate, and only one number is ever held. (4) (a + b)(a − b) works for any two numbers equally far from a middle; it helps only when the middle is easy to square. (5) Near 50, "25 plus the distance, then the distance squared as two digits" is exact while the distance is 9 or less; beyond that the square carries into the hundreds (62² = 3844) and the formula, not the shortcut, is what stays true. (6) Feynman and Bethe: only what the book's text says (fact sheet, source S1): the number is 48, the machine is the Marchant calculator, Bethe says 2300, then 2304; verbatim quotation is limited to two sentences; the book states Feynman's surprise, and Bethe's only through his question. The 1975 talk tells it with other words and is not mixed in. (7) x% of y = y% of x is always true and helps only when one of the two is a friendly percent. (8) Halving needs an even number; round then fix needs a round number close by. (9) The 1844 school book is a latest date for these tricks, never an origin, and nothing is said about "Vedic" mathematics. (10) No hype: every trick is shown failing to help somewhere, one quiz has nothing to grab, and paper or a calculator is said to be fine. |
