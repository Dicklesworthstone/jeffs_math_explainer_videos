| Topic (owner's words) | "let's make a new video about cool mental math tricks that are the most useful and practical and also WHY they work and include lots of quiz questions that we then answer after giving the people some time to think about it. The centerpiece should be "squaring numbers that end in 5" where you take the number to the left of the 5, call it n, and then compute n*(n+1) and then append "25" to the end of it. Also then we can lead into how to multiple and two 2-digit numbers in your head by breaking them into parts; like 23 times 46; that turns into 20 + 3 and 40 + 6 ; then you do the various cross products, ignoring zeros and then adding them back: 20*40+ 20*6 + 40*3 + 3*6 . For 20*40, just do 2*4 = 8 and then think about how many zeros it must have to make sense. The hardest part is remembering the 4 numbers and adding them up! Then there are other good tricks, like noticing when you can do (a + b)*(a - b), numbers that are close to 50 having easy mental math trick properties (the story Richard Feynman tells about Hans Bethe being surprised he didn't know that trick at Los Alamos, a good anedote for the video to include). You can think of a few other ones too (do web research), but they need to be fairly elementary, actually applicable and useful in real life, and doable by someone who isn't a genius." |
| Working title | **Mental Math Tricks That Actually Work (and Why)** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *Every Number Is Near Something You Know*; *Why Mental Math Tricks Work*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | A product is the area of a rectangle, and every trick in this video is one way to cut that rectangle into easy pieces or trade it for an equal one; see the picture once and you can square numbers ending in 5, multiply two-digit numbers, square numbers near 50 and do everyday percents in your head, and you can tell when a problem has nothing to grab and paper is the right tool. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | Eighty-five squared, done in your head the way school taught it on paper: two rows of digits to hold while you add them, and three carries along the way, worked from the small end, so the size of the answer arrives last. The trick needs one small product: eight times nine is seventy-two, then twenty-five. The school method is not wrong; it was built for paper, and paper does the remembering. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | A quiz before anything is taught: "eighty-five squared", a four-second countdown, then the answer, 7225, and the two steps that give it. It previews the payoff and the video's recurring device at once; then the title; then the honest failure of the paper method in chapter 01. |
| The pictures | (1) **The strip that moves.** Slice a strip 5 wide off the side of a square of 35 and lay it along the top: the square has become a rectangle 30 wide and 40 tall, three times four hundreds, with a 5 by 5 corner sticking out, the 25. This is the order's picture, and the same move, drawn the same way, is squares near 50 (a strip as wide as the distance: 48² is 46 by 50, Bethe's 2300, and a 2 by 2 corner) and, run backwards, (a + b)(a − b). The video ends on its exact form, a² = (a − b)(a + b) + b², which contains all three. (2) **The rectangle cut into parts**: 23 by 46 as four pieces, 20 × 40, 20 × 6, 3 × 40 and 3 × 6, added in a fixed order as a running total. The same rectangle cut in half and restacked is halving and doubling; with a sliver sliced off it is "round, then fix"; as a grid of cents read by rows or by columns it is x% of y = y% of x. Every trick in the video is one move on a rectangle. |
| Tricks, and why these | The owner's four: squares ending in 5 (the centrepiece); two-digit products by parts with a running total; (a + b)(a − b); squares near 50 with the Feynman and Bethe story. Four more from the research, in two short chapters, each judged by his three tests (elementary, useful in real life, doable by someone who isn't a genius) (default): **percents from a tenth** (10%, half of it, double it: tips, tax, discounts) and **the swap**, x% of y = y% of x; **halving and doubling** (×5 is ×10 then half, 14 × 35 = 7 × 70) and **round, then fix** (prices that end in .99), taught together as "get to a round number". "Round first", the estimate, is a habit taught at the start of the by-parts chapter and again at the end, not a trick of its own. Left out, with the reason: **the rule of 72** (useful, but it is the one candidate whose picture is not a rectangle, and an honest why, with where the rule drifts, costs about 105 s by the storyboard estimate; the video is already at the top of its length, so it would have to replace a chapter, or be a short video of its own; the research on it, down to Pacioli's 1494 page, is in the fact sheet); ×11 and products near 100 (rarely met in real life, and the same family as the core); casting out nines (no use since calculators; it misses every swap of two digits and about one random slip in nine); temperature and mile conversions (rules of thumb with no picture worth a chapter). |
| Order of the tricks | Squares ending in 5, then parts, as the owner said; then the two everyday chapters; then the middle, the Bethe story and near fifty; then the mixed round. | The "your turn" device | A card with the problem, a visible countdown ring of 4, 5 or 6 seconds by difficulty, the music alone and the narration silent, the line "pause for more time" on the card (spoken at the first quiz, before the four-piece quiz and before the mixed round; not on the cold open's card); at zero the answer lands, and the next line works it through with the trick in the same place on screen. Seventeen countdown quizzes: the cold open; two on squares ending in 5; two on parts; one on percents; two on round numbers; one on the middle; two near 50; a mixed round of four where the viewer must first pick which trick fits; a product with nothing to grab; and a last square that two tricks agree on. With the mixed round that is at least two for every trick, five for the centrepiece. Six more questions are asked without a ring (why the next number, which size, the swap, the middle, Bethe's step, above fifty). Every quiz number and answer comes from `facts.rs`, pinned against `oracle.py`. Each further quiz costs about 13 s. (default for the counts and the seconds) |
| Worked examples | 35² = 1225 (3 × 4 = 12, then 25; the picture: 30 × 40 + 25); 85² = 7225; 105² = 11025 (10 × 11 = 110); 23 × 46 = 800 + 120 + 120 + 18 = 1058, as a running total 800, 920, 1040, 1058; 15% of $48 = 4.80 + 2.40 = 7.20; 8% of $25 = 25% of $8 = $2; 5 × 86 = 430; 14 × 35 = 7 × 70 = 490; 4 × $19.99 = 80 − 0.04 = 79.96; 47 × 53 = 50² − 3² = 2491; 48² = 2304 and 53² = 2809 (25 minus or plus the distance for the hundreds, then the distance squared as two digits); 55² = 3025, by two tricks that give the same two numbers; 67 × 78 = 5226, the product no trick helps with (about 70 × 80 = 5600). The fact sheet has the full list with every quiz. |
| Spoken vocabulary (plan rule 21) | **trick** (never "hack", "shortcut", "secret" or "method"); **times** (never "multiplied by"); **squared**; **the number in front** (the digits left of the 5); **parts** (the tens and the ones a number breaks into) and **pieces** (the smaller rectangles); **block**, **strip** and **corner** (the three kinds of piece); **running total** and **floor**; **how big** (for the zeros) and **round first** (the estimate); **a tenth** (ten percent of a bill); **the middle** and **the distance**; **your turn** (every quiz); **round, then fix**; **double** and **halve**; **percent**; **paper** (the written method). Plain numbers are said in full and money keeps its "dollars"; the two-pair reading ("twenty-three oh four") is kept for squares near fifty. Never "genius", "human calculator", "instantly" or "lightning". The formal names appear once each, in a lower-third or on the last card: the distributive law, difference of squares, the area model. |
| Notation (screen) | Numbers before letters (plan rule 22). `n` for the digits in front of the 5 (the owner's letter), with the word label "the number in front"; the owner's `a` and `b` in `(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²`, in the precise line for squares near 50 and in the closing identity, always labelled "the middle" and "the distance" and never read aloud, so the letter a is not heard beside the number eight; × for times, never a dot or an asterisk; no thousands separator in a number being worked; money with two decimals; percents as `15%`. No Greek anywhere. |
| What the script must get right | (1) "Write 25 after it" means 100 × n × (n + 1) + 25, for every whole number n from 0 up (5² = 25 is the edge: zero times one); the picture is the reason and the program's check of every case to 99,995 is only a check. The trick is only as easy as n times the next number (135² needs 13 × 14). (2) The zeros come back by size: tens times tens are hundreds. (3) A running total is a floor that only rises, and between steps only one number is held; it is never said to have the right leading digits (800 against 1058 does not). (4) The middle trick works for any two numbers; it helps only when their middle is whole and easy to square and the distance is small. (5) Near 50, "25 plus the distance, then the distance squared as two digits" is exact while the distance is 9 or less; beyond that the square carries into the hundreds (62² = 3844) and the formula, not the reading, is what stays true. (6) Feynman and Bethe: only what the book's text says (fact sheet, source S1): the number is 48, the machine is a Marchant calculator, Bethe says 2300, then 2304; verbatim quotation is limited to two sentences; the book states Feynman's reaction, and Bethe's surprise only through his question. The 1975 talk tells it with other words and is not mixed in. (7) x% of y = y% of x is always true and helps only when one of the two is a friendly percent. (8) Halving and doubling pays only when doubling lands on a round number; round then fix only when the fix is small. (9) The 1844 school book is a latest date for three tricks, never an origin, and nothing is said about "Vedic" mathematics. (10) No hype: each trick's limit is shown where it is taught, one quiz has nothing to grab, and paper or a calculator is said to be fine; the estimate catches a wrong size, not a wrong digit. |
