# Brief — How to Learn a Secret About a Crowd Without Learning Each Person's Answer

| One-sentence takeaway | A coin that sometimes answers for you lets a survey count a secret behaviour in a crowd, exactly on average, while each answer stays deniable: never more than three times likelier from someone with the secret, a privacy that asking the same person again with fresh coins spends. |
| The promise | In the first 20 seconds: a direct question exposes whoever says yes, and a coin rule fixes that. It matters to anyone who collects data about people: the count survives the noise, the person's answer is only partly hidden, and the cost is precision. |
| Vocabulary (one word per idea) | class, copied, coin and second coin, heads and tails, tell the truth, answer, the survey, on average (storyboard, "Vocabulary") |
| Protected facts | `docs/protected.tsv` |
| The hook (where the obvious tool fails) | Asking directly: a yes is a confession, so anyone who copied has a reason to say no. No data is claimed about how often people lie; the failure is the exposure itself |
| The one picture | The coin rule as a table: rows are routes (the truth, the second coin), columns are answers (yes, no); students travel through it and land in cells, and the survey sees only the two column totals |
| Worked example | An invented class of 20, the 4 who copied in seats 2, 6, 13, 17. On average (every coin outcome equally often): 10 tell the truth (2 yes, 8 no), 10 get the second coin (5 yes, 5 no); 7 yes and 13 no; 7 − 20/4 = 2, doubled, 4 (`R-F1` to `R-F4`). Chapter 02: each student with all four coin outcomes, 80 in all; 12 of the 28 yes outcomes are a copier's, so a yes means 3/7 (not the 3/4 a copier says yes with), a no 4/52 = 1/13, from the class's 1/5 (`R-F11`, `R-F12`). Chapter 03: eight real seeded classes (estimates −4 to 14); over all 4²⁰ coin configurations the estimate averages 4, lands exactly on 4 about 1 time in 5 and below 0 about 1 in 11; the fixed class's standard deviation is √15 ≈ 3.87; a random sample adds p(1 − p)/n to the coins' 3/(4n), 91/16 times a truthful direct question at 1/5 (`R-F13`, `R-F14`, `R-F7`, `R-F8`). Chapter 04: one student asked three times with fresh coins, 64 outcomes; three yeses are 27 times as likely from someone who copied (27 outcomes against 1), and in the class 108 of 124 three-yes outcomes are a copier's, 27/31 against one yes's 3/7; a majority of the three is right 27/32 for either truth; one flip whose answer is repeated keeps three yeses at 3 (`R-F9`, `R-F10`, `R-F15`). Chapter 05: the first coin becomes a dial t, shown with a spinner of 4 slices over 8 outcomes; at 3/4 a yes is 7 of 8 against 1 of 8, 7 times either way, (1 + t)/(1 − t), while the coins' variance per person falls from 3/4 to 7/36, (1 − t²)/(4t²), so our fixed class of 20 misses by √(20 × 7/36) ≈ 1.97, about 2 people, not √15 ≈ 3.87, about 4; at 1/4, 5/3 and 15/4, a miss of √75 ≈ 8.66, about 9; at 1 no ratio bound and no variance, at 0 ratio 1 and no estimate (`R-F16`) |

| Assumptions | (1) Everyone follows the rule; the coins are fair and private (RAPPOR §1 names compliance as an assumption that may not hold for people). (2) Chapter 01's counts are average counts, said and carded as such; a real class scatters (`R-F7`). (3) The class is fixed: its scatter is the coins' alone (variance 3n/4 for the estimated count, 3/(4n) for the share). Independent draws, with replacement or from a large population, add p(1 − p)/n to the share's variance (n·p(1 − p) to the count's; `R-F8`); drawing all N = n without replacement adds nothing. Kept apart in chapter 03. (4) The privacy statement is about what one answer says about its own giver under this rule, nothing about other data. (5) Chapter 04's repeated answers are about one secret that does not change between asks; fresh coins are independent of each other, and a memoized answer is one flip resent. Nothing is claimed about repeated surveys in general, other questions, or RAPPOR's full design. (6) Chapter 05's dial keeps the second coin fair and changes only the chance t of telling the truth. Its variance is the coins' alone in one person's share of the estimate, (1 − t²)/(4t²): a fixed class's count has n times it, its share 1/n of it, and a sample adds p(1 − p)/n.
