| Working title | **The One Idea Behind Bridges, Concert Halls and Economies** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *Every Structure Has Its Own Patterns*; *Eigen Means Own*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | A steel bridge, the air in a room and an economy each have a few special patterns of their own, patterns the system hands back unchanged except for size; described in those patterns a tangle of equations in which everything pulls on everything falls apart into separate one-line problems, and that is why one calculation tells an engineer how a bridge sags and sways, which notes a room sings back and whether an economy can feed itself, and why knowing where the calculation stops is part of knowing it. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | London's Millennium Bridge, 10 June 2000. Its designers had checked how it would bounce under walkers, and their model had its sideways modes right to within 10% (Dallard et al. 2001). Under people walking, the deck swayed about 7 cm from side to side, and two days later the bridge was closed; the engineers' own paper calls it a loading effect "which had not been anticipated during design". What no code had asked was what would push along with those modes. The video opens on that paradox and closes on it with three questions: how strong is it, how does it want to move, and what will push along with it (R1 R1; the first draft said the engineers had to learn the second question, which the source contradicts). The same failure in miniature is chapter 02: move one of two carts joined by springs and you must push both, so no part can be worked out alone. |

| The pictures | (1) **The arrow that comes back along itself.** A system is a rule that turns one list of numbers into another: how far each part moves into how hard each part pushes back. Draw both lists as arrows. Sweep the first arrow round a circle and the second swings ahead of it or behind it, and lines up with it in only a few directions. Those directions are the system's own patterns (eigenvectors), and the stretch along each is its number (eigenvalue). For two carts on three springs the directions are "both the same way" (stretch 1) and "opposite ways" (stretch 3). The same test is run on the truss, the air and the economy (R1 C1). (2) **The split, on a ladder of own patterns.** Any push is a mix of the own patterns, each pattern answers by itself, and the answers add up: two tangled equations become two one-line divisions. The same ladder is the truss's 21 patterns (the softest alone lands within half a percent of the sag at midspan, while the member forces need many more), a vibrating structure (each mode one oscillator with one frequency), the air in a room (each pattern a standing wave, one tone each) and an economy (each round multiplies each pattern by its eigenvalue, so a lopsided order swings onto the even mix and the largest eigenvalue decides). The video ends on the split of chapter 04 run forwards, which is K = QΛQᵀ, and on the places it stops applying. |

| Worked examples | **Two carts, three springs** (each spring 1 N per cm): moves (1, 0) need pushes (2, −1); (1, 1) need (1, 1); (1, −1) need (3, −3); a push of (1, 0) moves the carts (2/3, 1/3), found as half of each pattern, each divided by its stiffness. **A Pratt truss**: 24 m span, six panels of 4 m, 4 m deep, 12 joints, 21 members, steel E = 200 GPa, chords and end posts 6,000 mm², web members 3,000 mm²: K is 21 by 21 with 97 of its 441 entries not zero; 100 kN on one joint moves all 21 directions (4.7 mm down there, 1.3 mm sideways at the roller); 100 kN on each of the five deck joints sags the middle 18.0 mm (span/1,336) and puts 450 kN of squeeze in the top chord (75 N/mm², under A36 steel's 250); K's eigenvalues run from 3.12 to 1,226 MN/m, and the softest pattern alone lands within half a percent of that sag (99.6%) while the worst member force is still off by 82 kN; with a 4-tonne deck mass at each joint the lowest modes ring at 5.9, 11.8 and 14.9 Hz, and 4.3 Hz with the deck twice as heavy. **Rooms**: a bathroom 2.5 by 1.6 by 2.4 m has its first tones at 68.6 and 71.5 Hz; a 3 m cube has three modes at the same 57.2 Hz; a box 45 by 23 by 18 m (18,630 m³, 99.4% of Boston Symphony Hall's volume) has its first mode at 3.8 Hz, 2,246 modes below 100 Hz and 1,964,040 below 1 kHz, and a Schroeder frequency of 20.2 Hz at a reverberation time of 1.9 s; a room 4.8 by 4 by 2.6 m crosses over at 200 Hz with 67 modes below, no two on one tone; an attic studio 6 by 3 m with a sloping ceiling, cut into 232 cells, has its first modes at 29.8, 56.1 and 59.9 Hz. **An economy of two industries** (food and tools; a model): making ten of each uses up seven of each, so the largest eigenvalue is 0.7 and its eigenvector the even mix; delivering 24 of each takes 80 of each, in rounds of 24, 16.8, 11.76 and so on; a lopsided order of 30 and 10 is 18 of the even mix and 4 of the other pattern, its rounds swing onto the even mix, and it takes 75 and 50; with an appetite of 1.1 the rounds grow and no plan works. **The factory** (one picture in the coda): corners (0, 0), (0, 40), (40, 20), (50, 0); profits 0, 280, 460, 400. Every number is in the fact sheet with its key. |

| Notation (screen) | Numbers before letters (plan rule 22). `K` the stiffness table, `u` the moves, `f` the pushes: `K u = f`. An own pattern `q` with `K q = λ q`; the split `K = Q Λ Qᵀ` and `u = Σ (qᵢ·f / λᵢ) qᵢ`. Vibration: `K φ = ω² M φ` with `M` the mass table; frequencies are shown as numbers in Hz, never as a letter (f is the push). A member's spring: `k = EA/L` (A is the cross-section, the only use of the letter A; member force `N`). The room: `c` the speed of sound, sides `Lx, Ly, Lz`, whole numbers `nx, ny, nz`. The economy: `C` the input table, `x` what is made, `d` what is delivered: `x = C x + d`. The general form appears once, in the coda, as `C = V Λ V⁻¹`, the letters agreed with Video J. No θ, ρ or ε (they draw wrongly at the pin): the largest eigenvalue is written λ with a subscript and never called ρ. |
| What the script must get right | (1) An eigenvector is a direction: any non-zero multiple is the same one, and its sign is free. (2) A symmetric table always has a full set of own patterns at right angles, with real numbers; a table that is not symmetric may have fewer directions (a shear has one) or none in the real plane (a quarter turn). The video's structures are symmetric; the economy's table is not, and what saves it is that its entries are not negative (Perron, Frobenius). (3) K is positive definite only once the supports stop the truss moving as a whole: unsupported, it has exactly three eigenvalues of zero. (4) The sum over modes is exact with all 21 and approximate with fewer; deflection converges fast and member forces slowly (computed, and said). (5) For one static load real programs factor K and never compute its eigenvectors; modes earn their keep when things move. Said, in a "for programmers" aside. (6) In `Kφ = ω²Mφ` the eigenvalue is the frequency squared, and the modes are at right angles only when mass is counted (φᵢᵀMφⱼ = 0); the model has no damping. (7) Tacoma Narrows, 7 November 1940: the motion that destroyed it was one twisting mode at about 0.2 Hz, fed by the wind's own reaction to the twisting (flutter); it was not a gust keeping time with the bridge (Billah and Scanlan 1991). Never "resonance" without that qualification. (8) Millennium Bridge: past a critical number of walkers the sideways sway fed on itself; never "people fell into step and that caused it" (the 2005 and 2021 papers disagree on that, and the wording stays true under both). (9) Room modes are for hard walls and the formula is for a box; pressure is greatest at the walls and corners. Three of the most admired halls are shoeboxes (Beranek), so a box is never called bad; what is bad is a cube, or sides in simple ratios, in a small room. (10) The Schroeder frequency is a crossover, not a wall, and for a hall it sits near the bottom of hearing; above it modes still exist but overlap. Sabine's formula is statistics, not modes; the constant 0.161 is the modern metric one (his own was 0.164), and he advised on Boston's hall, he did not design it. (11) "Eigen" is German for "own"; Helmholtz wrote of the *Eigentöne* of a body of air in 1863 and Hilbert named *Eigenwert* in 1904 for integral equations: the same word, and nothing is claimed about one borrowing from the other. (12) Leontief: with no negative entries, the economy can meet every demand exactly when the largest eigenvalue is below one (Hawkins and Simon's condition), and then the rounds add up to (I − C)⁻¹d. The table is a model with two industries; his first real tables had 44 sectors. (13) The factory problem is linear programming: its answer sits at a corner and no eigenvector finds it. (14) FrankenSim is cited for what it has (the generalized eigenproblem solver, the rectangular-cavity formula, the dense Jacobi kernel, steel frames) and never for what it has not (no pin-jointed truss by direct stiffness, no room acoustics, no input-output model). (15) No hype: one decomposition, three places it rules and three where it stops. |
