| Topic (owner's words) | "it's about how you can take an unfair coin and use it in a clever way to simulate a fair coin by using double tosses and culling the sample space to remove HH and TT and then instead of one person calling heads, they can call HT, and the other person chooses TH. Since the trials are independent, the probabilities commute and HT and TH are equally likely even if H by itself has an 80% probability instead of 50%! And then also how this idea could generalize to more complex scenarios, like unfair dice." |
| Working title | **How to Make a Fair Coin Out of an Unfair One** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *The Fair Coin Inside Every Unfair One*; *Heads-Tails or Tails-Heads*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | Toss the unfair coin twice and let the order of two different results decide: heads-then-tails and tails-then-heads are exactly equally likely whatever the coin's chance of heads, because the same results in a different order always have the same chance; and that one fact also turns a bent coin or a loaded die into a fair die, and measures how much fairness an unfair source holds. |
| The hook (where intuition fails) | One coin, and it lands heads four times in five. Alice knows that; Bob only suspects it. "Call it" hands the bet to whoever knows the coin best, and letting Bob call does not help him, because he cannot tell which side is the lucky one. Can that same coin still settle the bet exactly fairly, whatever either of them knows? (R1: the first draft had neither player knowing the coin, and then a blind call is already a fair guess; Maya and Ruth both caught it.) |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The lopsided coin lands heads again and again, a bar under the tosses leaning far to one side; under "perfectly fairly, using nothing but this coin" a second, level bar grows beneath it, captioned "the same coin": the payoff shown as a paradox. Then the title; the honest failure of "just call it" (791 heads in 1,000 seeded tosses) opens the chapter. |
| The pictures | **One picture, the unit square cut by the chances** (the first toss cuts the width, the second the height). Heads-then-tails and tails-then-heads are the two off-diagonal blocks, p by (1 − p) and (1 − p) by p: one is the other flipped across the diagonal, so their areas are equal wherever the cut slides. The same square shows the price (the grey), and every way the trick breaks: a second coin moves one cut and not the other; a coin with memory puts a step in the second cut. For three tosses the square gains a third side: a cube whose three two-heads slabs are one slab turned three ways ("same counts, same chance"). Pascal's triangle is what is left of the picture when there are too many tosses to draw: the patterns sorted by their counts, each entry a fair choice among that many (row 2's middle 2 is the trick, row 4's middle 6 is a die). For a loaded die the square is cut six ways, and for three rolls the cube returns: the six bricks with one side of each length are one brick turned six ways. |
| Worked examples | the 80% coin: blocks of 16, 4, 4 and 1 small squares out of 25; a pair decides 32% of the time; 6.25 tosses on average (4 for a fair coin, about 101 at 99%); 1,000 seeded games: 494 to 506, every pair drawn as a dot in its block; an 80% coin then a fair coin: 40% against 10%, the same 80% back; a coin that repeats its last result 80% of the time: after two heads, heads-then-tails is four times as likely as tails-then-heads; three tosses: three slabs of 12.8% each; four tosses with two heads: 6 patterns; 24,000 tosses made 949 rolls of a fair die; a die loaded so six comes up half the time (5, 5, 10, 10, 20, 50 percent): 3,000 pairs of rolls split 1,021 to 1,020; a four, a five and a six in six orders are six bricks of 1/10 by 1/5 by 1/2; fair tosses per hundred unfair ones: 16 by the trick, just over 72 at most. |

| What the script must get right | (1) What the trick needs: both tosses of a pair have the same chance of heads and neither affects the other. "Independent" is the name; the owner's "the probabilities commute" is the multiplication p(1 − p) = (1 − p)p. (2) "Every coin" means every chance strictly between 0 and 1: a coin that cannot land both ways never decides. (3) The game ends with probability one but has no longest length, and no rule that stops after a fixed number of tosses is exactly fair for every coin (edge test E-X1). (4) 6.25 is an average. (5) Von Neumann's own rule takes the first toss of the differing pair (heads-tails counts as heads); his words are quoted verbatim only; the printed text is George Forsythe's summary of a 1949 talk, issued in 1951. (6) Real coins: a coin flipped and caught cannot be biased by weighting it (Gelman and Nolan 2002); a spun or bounced one can; the 80% coin is a model and is labelled so. Never say "spun pennies land tails 80% of the time" (a journalist's paraphrase of Diaconis, with no data behind it that we could find). (7) Diaconis, Holmes and Montgomery: the same side it started, about 51%, not "heads 51%"; the 350,757-flip study's interval is a Bayesian credible interval. (8) Memory: the first pair of a long run is still fair; what breaks is the pair after one that was thrown away. The cure for a coin that remembers one toss is Samuelson's (1968): pair up only the tosses that follow a head. Using every toss needs Blum's late announcement (FOCS 1984; the 1986 journal text was not seen); the eager version is wrong. The cure is fair when played to the end, and leans under a deadline. (9) The entropy bounds the average over a long run (a single run of 100 tosses can give more than 72); Peres's and Elias's rules approach it and never reach it; Peres reuses two things, which pairs matched and what the matching pairs showed, and naming only the thrown-away pairs is wrong (it levels off near 18 per hundred). (10) "Extractor": randomness extraction "goes back to von Neumann" (Vadhan); never "the first extractor". Intel's 1999 generator used a von Neumann corrector (past tense); NIST's final SP 800-90B does not list the method, so never "NIST-approved". (11) For dice, the orders of values that all differ; ties are thrown away. |
