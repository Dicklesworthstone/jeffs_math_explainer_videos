| Topic (owner's words) | "it's about how you can take an unfair coin and use it in a clever way to simulate a fair coin by using double tosses and culling the sample space to remove HH and TT and then instead of one person calling heads, they can call HT, and the other person chooses TH. Since the trials are independent, the probabilities commute and HT and TH are equally likely even if H by itself has an 80% probability instead of 50%! And then also how this idea could generalize to more complex scenarios, like unfair dice." |
| Working title | **How to Make a Fair Coin Out of an Unfair One** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *The Fair Coin Inside Every Unfair One*; *Heads-Tails or Tails-Heads*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | Toss the unfair coin twice and let the order of two different results decide: heads-then-tails and tails-then-heads are exactly equally likely whatever the coin's chance of heads, because the same results in a different order always have the same chance; and that one fact also turns a bent coin or a loaded die into a fair die, and measures how much fairness an unfair source holds. |
| The hook (where intuition fails) | One coin, and it lands heads four times in five. "Call it" hands the bet to whoever says heads 80% of the time, and neither player knows the coin's real chance, so they cannot correct for it. Can that same coin still settle the bet exactly fairly? |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The lopsided coin lands heads again and again (791 of 1,000 in the seeded run); the question on a card; then the title. The honest failure of "just call it" opens chapter 01. |
| The pictures | (1) **The unit square cut at p on both axes** (the first toss cuts the width, the second the height): heads-tails and tails-heads are the two off-diagonal rectangles, p by (1 − p) and (1 − p) by p, the same rectangle mirrored across the diagonal, so their areas are equal wherever the cut slides. The same square shows every way the trick breaks: a second coin moves one cut and not the other; a coin with memory cuts the two columns at different heights. (2) **Pascal's triangle as counts of patterns**: the patterns of n tosses, sorted by how many heads they have; every pattern in a group has the same chance, so each entry is a fair choice among that many patterns (row 2's middle 2 is the coin trick, row 4's middle 6 is a die). One level up, for a loaded die, the unit cube cut by the die's chances on three axes: the six bricks with one side of each length are the same brick turned six ways. |
| Worked examples | the 80% coin: areas 0.64, 0.16, 0.16, 0.04; a pair decides 32% of the time; 6.25 tosses on average (4 for a fair coin, about 101 at 99%); 1,000 seeded games: 494 to 506; an 80% coin then a fair coin: 0.40 against 0.10, the same 80% back; a coin that repeats its last face 80% of the time: after two heads, heads-tails is four times as likely as tails-heads; four tosses with two heads: 6 patterns at 0.0256 each; a die loaded so six comes up half the time (5, 5, 10, 10, 20, 50 percent): two rolls tie 31.5% of the time, three rolls all differ 32.6% of the time, six orders at 5.43% each; fair tosses per hundred unfair ones: 16 by the trick, 72 at most. |

| What the script must get right | (1) What the trick needs: both tosses of a pair have the same chance of heads and neither affects the other. "Independent" is the name; the owner's "the probabilities commute" is the multiplication p(1 − p) = (1 − p)p. (2) "Every coin" means every chance strictly between 0 and 1: a coin that cannot land both ways never decides. (3) The game ends with probability one but has no longest length, and no rule that stops after a fixed number of tosses is exactly fair for every coin (edge test E-X1). (4) 6.25 is an average. (5) Von Neumann's own rule takes the first toss of the differing pair (heads-tails counts as heads); his words are quoted verbatim only; the printed text is George Forsythe's summary of a 1949 talk, issued in 1951. (6) Real coins: a coin flipped and caught cannot be biased by weighting it (Gelman and Nolan 2002); a spun or bounced one can; the 80% coin is a model and is labelled so. Never say "spun pennies land tails 80% of the time" (a journalist's paraphrase of Diaconis, with no data behind it that we could find). (7) Diaconis, Holmes and Montgomery: the same side it started, about 51%, not "heads 51%"; the 350,757-flip study's interval is a Bayesian credible interval. (8) Memory: the first pair of a long run is still fair; what breaks is the pair after one that was thrown away. The cure for a coin that remembers one toss is Samuelson's (1968): pair up only the tosses that follow a head. Using every toss needs Blum's (1986) late announcement; the eager version is wrong. (9) The entropy is a limit for the long run; Peres's and Elias's rules approach it and never reach it. (10) "Extractor": randomness extraction "goes back to von Neumann" (Vadhan); never "the first extractor". Intel's 1999 generator used a von Neumann corrector (past tense); NIST's final SP 800-90B does not list the method, so never "NIST-approved". (11) For dice, the orders of values that all differ; ties are thrown away. |
