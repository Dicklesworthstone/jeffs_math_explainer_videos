In 1974 Leslie Lamport published a way for programs to take turns using only memory that each of them writes alone: take a ticket bigger than any you see, break ties by customer number, and raise a flag while you choose. Its proof never trusts a read that overlaps a write, so the algorithm survives memory that returns garbage. This video builds the bakery algorithm, proves it, and shows what it costs on a real computer.

What it costs
The customers are fixed in advance, with one flag and one ticket slate each. An uncontended entry reads each other customer's slate twice and her flag once. Waiting means spinning and re-reading. A customer frozen with her choosing flag raised stalls the others after at most one more turn each, and a dead customer's slate stays set until something resets it. Run with plain loads and stores on today's processors, it can fail: use sequentially consistent atomics, or fences placed for your architecture, and 64-bit tickets.
