| Working title | **What Does It Mean to Raise e to a Matrix?** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *e to the Power of a Matrix*; *The Function That Turns Rules into Results*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | The exponential is the machine that turns a rule for tiny steps into where you end up: put a number in and you get growth, put i in and you get turning, put a matrix in and you get the motion of a whole linked system (salt between tanks, heat across a network, chance, rotation), and the matrices you may put in, with the motions that come out, are exactly a Lie algebra and its Lie group. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | "e to a matrix" cannot mean "e multiplied by itself a matrix number of times", and the obvious repair, raise e to each entry, fails on the simplest test there is: the all-zeros matrix. e to the zero should be the thing that changes nothing, the identity matrix; entry by entry it gives a matrix of four ones, which flattens every arrow onto one line. The guess also breaks the rule that makes exponentials worth having (running for time s and then for time t is running for s + t). The failure is exact arithmetic on a 2 by 2, nothing cherry-picked. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | A quiz before anything is taught: e raised to the 2 by 2 matrix with rows (0, −1) and (1, 0), a five-second countdown, then the answer, a rotation by exactly one radian, with the plane turning under it. "By the end, you'll see why it couldn't be anything else." Then the title, then the honest failure of the entry-by-entry guess. |
| The one picture | **A rule for tiny steps, followed.** A plane of dots, each carrying a small teal arrow that says where it is headed right now (the rule: the matrix applied to the dot's position); follow the arrows for time t and every dot lands somewhere new (the result: e^{tA}, in gold). The same picture is compound interest on a number line ((1 + r/n)^n: n tiny steps of growth), turning in the complex plane (each tiny step sideways: a circle), Euler's method for a system ((I + tA/n)^n), salt moving between two tanks, heat and chance spreading on a network, and, at the end, in its rigorous form: a straight line of tiny steps in the Lie algebra wrapped by exp onto a curve in the Lie group. |
| Worked examples (default) | (1) **Compounding to e**: one dollar at 100% a year, compounded 1, 2, 4, 12, 365 times: 2, 2.25, 2.44, 2.61, 2.71, settling at 2.71828. (2) **The series**, earned term by term from "its rate equals its size": 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + …; at x = 1 the running total is 2, 2.5, 2.667, 2.708, 2.717, 2.71828: the same number. (3) **The quarter turn** J, rows (0, −1), (1, 0): J² = −I, J³ = −J, J⁴ = I, so the series splits into cosine and sine and e^{tJ} is the rotation by t; e^{J} has entries 0.540 and ±0.841. (4) **Does it settle?** e^{5J}: the terms grow to 26 before the factorial wins, the running total swings out past 15 and still comes home to (0.284, −0.959) on the unit circle. (5) **Two tanks**, 100 litres each, 10 litres a minute pumped each way, 100 grams of salt in the first: one big step of ten minutes puts all the salt in the other tank (absurd), ten steps give 55.4 grams, a thousand give 56.75, and e^{10A} gives 56.77 at once; it settles at 50 and 50. (6) **A four-node network** (a triangle with a tail): powers of its matrix count walks, e^A adds every walk with weight one over k factorial; with the Laplacian, e^{−tL} is heat spreading and also the chance of finding a random hopper at each node, settling at a quarter each, hub or not. (7) **One letter**: on two nodes, e^{−tL} settles at half and half, and e^{−itL} sloshes back and forth for ever (a two-state quantum system). (8) **The rule that breaks**: two shears A and B; e^A e^B, e^B e^A and e^{A+B} are three different matrices, and the gap for short times is half of t² times AB − BA. (9) **A series that fails on a computer**: Moler and Van Loan's 2 by 2, whose terms reach nine million before cancelling to numbers near one; in 32-bit arithmetic the series returns four numbers of which none is right to two digits and one has the wrong sign, and halving the matrix eight times first (scaling and squaring) gets all four right to three decimal places. (10) **Rotations**: three independent spins in three dimensions, six in four; 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 for the Standard Model's SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). Every number is computed in `facts.rs` and recomputed in `oracle.py`. |

| What the script must get right | (1) The three definitions are one function: "rate equals size, starting at one", the limit of (1 + x/n)^n and the series; the series is shown to be its own rate term by term, the limit is shown to be the same tiny-step rule, and uniqueness is stated with its one-line reason in a lower-third. (2) `y′ = ry` has the solutions `y(0)e^{rt}` and no others; `x′ = Ax` has `x(t) = e^{tA}x(0)`. (3) The matrix series converges for every square matrix, real or complex: the size of A^k is at most the size of A to the k, so the terms sit under those of the number series for e to the size of A; "settles" means each entry settles. (4) e^{tA} is the flow: e^{(s+t)A} = e^{sA}e^{tA} always (A commutes with itself). (5) (A^k) counts walks, repeats allowed, not paths; e^A is Estrada and Hatano's communicability (2008); heat uses L = D − A (Kondor and Lafferty's form; Chung's Laplacian is the normalized one). (6) For the hopper that leaves along each link at rate one, the long-run chance is equal at every node, not larger at the hub. (7) Quantum: only "a two-state system, with ħ set to one"; e^{−iHt} keeps total probability one. (8) **The order's "only when A and B commute" is true for all times, not for one pair**: e^{t(A+B)} = e^{tA}e^{tB} for every t exactly when AB = BA; a single pair can satisfy e^{A+B} = e^A e^B without commuting (three full turns about x, four about y, five about the diagonal: all the identity). The script says "when they commute it holds; when they don't, it can fail", and the exact statement is on screen. (9) The exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is a rotation, and every rotation arises this way (SO(n) is compact and connected); exp is not one-to-one (a full turn is the identity). (10) The Standard Model line is scoped: its forces are described by a gauge theory with group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 generators matching 8 gluons, W⁺, W⁻, Z and the photon; the fine print (a possible finite quotient that experiments cannot yet tell apart) is in the description. Nothing is claimed about gravity or about why those groups. (11) Computing: the series is correct mathematics and a poor algorithm; Moler and Van Loan name no single best method ("perhaps three or four candidates"), and scaling and squaring with Padé approximants is what MATLAB and SciPy use today (Higham 2005; Al-Mohy and Higham 2009). Their printed wrong answer came from IBM 370 arithmetic and is quoted as theirs; the wrong answer the video computes is from its own 32-bit run. (12) History, only as verified: Euler's *Introductio* (1748) has the series, the limit and e^{v√−1} = cos v + √−1 sin v; Jacob Bernoulli's compound-interest question was published in 1690; Laguerre (1867) defined e to a matrix by the series and noted that e^X e^Y = e^{X+Y} fails in general; Peano (1887–88) used it to solve x′ = Rx; Sophus Lie (1842–1899). |
