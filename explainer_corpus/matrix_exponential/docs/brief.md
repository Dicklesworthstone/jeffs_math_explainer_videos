| Working title | **What Does It Mean to Raise e to a Matrix?** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *e to the Power of a Matrix*; *The Function That Turns Rules into Results*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | The exponential is the machine that turns a rule for tiny steps into where you end up: put a number in and you get growth, put i in and you get turning, put a matrix in and you get the motion of a whole linked system (salt between tanks, heat across a network, chance, rotation); and the spins you may put in, with the rotations that come out, are a Lie algebra and the Lie group it generates. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | "e to a matrix" cannot mean "e multiplied by itself a matrix number of times", and the obvious repair, raise e to each entry, fails on the simplest test there is: the all-zeros matrix. e to the zero should be the thing that changes nothing, the identity matrix; entry by entry it gives a matrix of four ones, which flattens every arrow onto one line. The guess also breaks the rule that makes exponentials worth having (running for time s and then for time t is running for s + t). The failure is exact arithmetic on a 2 by 2, nothing cherry-picked. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The question, e raised to the 2 by 2 matrix with rows (0, −1) and (1, 0), then at once its answer, a rotation by exactly one radian, with the central picture already running (every dot of the plane follows a small arrow at right angles to its position). "By the end, you'll see why it couldn't be anything else." The title lands at about twenty seconds, then what a matrix is, then the honest failure of the entry-by-entry guess. The question comes back as the last quiz (16_3), when the viewer can answer it in one sentence: the rule moves every point at a speed equal to its distance from the centre, so one unit of time is an arc as long as the radius. (Revised after review R1: the first storyboard put a countdown on the opening question, which nobody can yet answer.) |
| The one picture | **A rule for tiny steps, followed.** A matrix is a machine: feed it an arrow and it hands back an arrow. Read the arrow that comes out as a velocity, and the matrix is a rule for motion: a plane of dots, each carrying a small teal arrow that says where it is headed right now; follow the arrows for a time t and every dot is carried somewhere (the result: e^{tA}, in gold). The same picture is compound interest on a number line ((1 + r/n)^n: n tiny steps of growth), turning in the complex plane (each tiny step sideways: a circle), Euler's method for a system ((I + tA/n)^n), the pair of salt readings of two tanks as one dot on that plane, heat and chance spreading on a network, and, at the end, in its rigorous form for the opening example: a line of tiny steps (the multiples of the quarter turn) wrapped by exp onto a circle of rotations. |
| Worked examples (default) | (1) **Compounding to e**: one dollar at 100% a year, compounded 1, 2, 4, 12, 365 times: 2, 2.25, 2.44, 2.61, 2.71, settling at 2.71828. (2) **The series**, earned term by term from "its rate equals its size": 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + …; at x = 1 the running total is 2, 2.5, 2.667, 2.708, 2.717, 2.71828: the same number. (3) **The quarter turn** J, rows (0, −1), (1, 0): J² = −I, J³ = −J, J⁴ = I, so the series splits into cosine and sine and e^{tJ} is the rotation by t; e^{J} has entries 0.540 and ±0.841. (4) **Does it settle?** e^{5J}: the terms grow to 26 before the factorial wins, the running total swings out past 21 and still comes home to (0.284, −0.959) on the unit circle; a second matrix (three times a shear) shows terms sitting strictly under their ceilings. (5) **Two tanks**, 100 litres each, 10 litres a minute pumped each way, 100 grams of salt in the first: one big step of ten minutes puts all the salt in the other tank (absurd), ten steps give 55.4 grams, a thousand give 56.75, and e^{10A} gives 56.77 at once; it settles at 50 and 50. (6) **A four-node network** (a triangle with a tail): powers of its matrix count walks, e^A adds every walk with weight one over k factorial; with the Laplacian, e^{−tL} is heat spreading and also the chance of finding a random hopper (one that leaves along each link at rate one) at each node, levelling at a quarter each; then the same on a grid network of four hundred nodes, with 4,988,731,519,200 walks of forty steps between opposite corners. (7) **One letter**: on two nodes, e^{−tL} settles at half and half, and e^{−itL} sloshes back and forth for ever (a two-state quantum system). (8) **The rule that breaks**: two shears A and B; e^A e^B, e^B e^A and e^{A+B} are three different matrices; a square taken round the loop A, B, undo A, undo B does not come back, and what is left over is −t²(AB − BA) for short times. (9) **A series that fails on a computer**: Moler and Van Loan's 2 by 2, whose terms reach nine million before cancelling to numbers near one; in 32-bit arithmetic the series returns four numbers of which none is right to two digits and one has the wrong sign, and halving the matrix eight times first (scaling and squaring) gets all four right to three decimal places. (10) **Rotations**: three independent spins in three dimensions (one for each pair of axes; four axes would make six); a cube tipped about x, about y and back again, twenty times over, creeps 45.7° about the third axis; 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 for the Standard Model's SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). Every number is computed in `facts.rs` and recomputed in `oracle.py`. |

| Notation (screen), shared with Videos G and K | Numbers before letters (plan rule 22). `e^{tA}`, read "e to the t A", with the time t written first; matrices `A`, `B`, the identity `I`, the quarter turn `J`; the state `x(t)` and the system `x′ = Ax`; the rate `r` (growth: `y′ = ry`), the number of tiny steps `n`, the term number `k` (so `A^k/k!` and walks of length k, as in the order); the angle of a turn is the time `t` (`e^{it}`): the engine at this pin draws θ as ϑ, and one letter for "how long you turned" is the idea anyway. `i` is only ever the square root of minus one: no row and column subscripts, entries are pointed at. `L` the Laplacian, `H` in `e^{−iHt}`; the Markov name `e^{tQ}` appears once, in a lower-third. `[A, B] = AB − BA`. | What the script must get right | (1) The three definitions are one function: "rate equals size, starting at one", the limit of (1 + x/n)^n and the series; the series is shown to be its own rate term by term, the limit is shown to be the same tiny-step rule, and uniqueness is stated with its one-line reason in a lower-third. (2) `y′ = ry` has the solutions `y(0)e^{rt}` and no others; `x′ = Ax` has `x(t) = e^{tA}x(0)`. (3) The matrix series converges for every square matrix, real or complex: the size of A^k is at most the size of A to the k, so the terms sit under those of the number series for e to the size of A; "settles" means each entry settles. (4) e^{tA} is the flow: e^{(s+t)A} = e^{sA}e^{tA} always (A commutes with itself). (5) (A^k) counts walks, repeats allowed, not paths; e^A is Estrada and Hatano's communicability (2008); heat uses L = D − A (Kondor and Lafferty's form; Chung's Laplacian is the normalized one). (6) For the hopper that leaves along each link at rate one, the long-run chance is equal at every node; for a hopper that takes one hop per tick it is larger at the busy node (3/8). The screen says which hopper it shows. (7) Quantum: only "a two-state system, with ħ set to one"; e^{−iHt} keeps total probability one. (8) **The order's "only when A and B commute" is true for all times, not for one pair**: e^{t(A+B)} = e^{tA}e^{tB} for every t exactly when AB = BA; a single pair can satisfy e^{A+B} = e^A e^B without commuting (three full turns about x, four about y, five about a tilted axis: all the identity). The script says "when they commute it holds; when they don't, it can fail", and the exact statement is on screen. (9) The exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is a rotation, and every rotation arises this way (SO(n) is compact and connected); exp is not one-to-one (a full turn is the identity). (10) The Standard Model line is scoped: its forces are described by a gauge theory with group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 generators matching 8 gluons, W⁺, W⁻, Z and the photon; the fine print (a possible finite quotient that experiments cannot yet tell apart) is in the description. Nothing is claimed about gravity or about why those groups. (11) Computing: the series is correct mathematics and a poor algorithm; Moler and Van Loan name no single best method ("perhaps three or four candidates"), and scaling and squaring with Padé approximants is what MATLAB and SciPy use today (Higham 2005; Al-Mohy and Higham 2009). Their printed wrong answer came from IBM 370 arithmetic and is quoted as theirs; the wrong answer the video computes is from its own 32-bit run. (12) History, only as verified: Euler's *Introductio* (1748) has the series, the limit and e^{v√−1} = cos v + √−1 sin v; Jacob Bernoulli's compound-interest question was published in 1690; Laguerre (1867) defined e to a matrix by the series and noted that e^X e^Y = e^{X+Y} fails in general; Peano (1888; first in Italian, 1887) used it to solve x′ = Rx; Sophus Lie (1842–1899). |
