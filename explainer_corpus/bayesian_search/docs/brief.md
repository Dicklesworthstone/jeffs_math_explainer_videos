| Working title | **How to Find a Lost Submarine** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *The Map That Learns From Finding Nothing*; *Where to Look for Something Lost at Sea*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | Keep a map of where the lost thing could be; search where the next hour is most likely to find it, which is the chance it is there times the chance you would see it; and when a search comes back empty, redraw the map: the cell you searched loses chance, every other cell gains it, and the map is only as honest as your estimate of how well you looked. |
| The opening question | The owner's subject line, in his words, as the cold open: "A nuclear sub has gone missing. Men are trapped, and time is running out. How can we possibly find them in time?" Then the truth, in chapter 01 and not later (the order's "honesty about the hook"): in May 1968 the Navy did not know the *Scorpion*'s fate and searched the Atlantic as if lives might be saved; she had sunk on 22 May, five days before she was due home, in more than 10,000 feet of water; all 99 men were lost; the wreck was found on 28 October. Nothing in the video implies the crew could have been saved. The question stays, because the same method is used where hours do matter. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | Two obvious plans, both failing on the real numbers. **Search everywhere:** the towed sled saw a strip about 200 feet wide; passing once over the 150 square miles of the search grid would take about 4,600 sea miles of track (model), four and a half times what the whole search towed (1,026). **Search the likeliest place first:** in the video's four-cell sea the likeliest cell gives a 10% chance of a find and the second likeliest gives 20%, because the likeliest cell is a canyon where a search sees one wreck in four. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The question on a plain dark stage, as a premise. Then the picture that answers it, shown for two and a half seconds with no words: the 1968 map's dots on its lattice, a search crossing the brightest cell, most of that cell's dots going out, and every other cell deepening. Then the title; then the truth about the *Scorpion* and the failure of "search everywhere" in chapter 02. |
| The one picture | **The map is a cloud of dots, and each dot is one place the lost thing could be.** A search goes over a cell and puts out the dots it would have found. If the search came back empty, the truth is one of the dots still lit, so the new chance of each cell is its share of the dots that are left. That one move is Bayes' rule: the searched cell loses dots, no other cell does, and every other cell's share rises. The picture is not a teaching device laid over the method; it is the method. The 1968 map was 10,000 simulated submarines counted by cell, and the Coast Guard's search planner moves thousands of simulated drifting people and boats and reweights them after every empty search. The video ends on the rigorous form of the same picture: the two-line update and the rule for where to search next, with the theorem that no other order of searches does better. |
| The devices that repeat | (1) **The dots** (above). (2) **The four-cell sea**: one small, made-up map that every hand calculation uses; its lost ship is "it", never "she". (3) **Your turn**: a quiz with a visible countdown, as in Video F, but as a frame round the live picture and never a card over it: the question, a ring that drains over 6 seconds with the narration silent, "pause for more time", then the answer worked where the picture stands (default for the seconds). (4) **The 1968 map**: the numbers Richardson and Stone printed, as a grid the video returns to three times: first as the payoff previewed, then as the map a model search runs over, then with what really happened. |
| Worked example (every number by hand) | The four-cell sea, 100 dots. Two stories of what happened, weighted 60 and 40, put 40, 30, 20 and 10 dots in cells A, B, C and D. A is a canyon: a search there sees the wreck 1 time in 4; in B, C and D, 2 times in 3. Chance of a find = dots × chance of seeing: A 10, B 20, C 13⅓, D 6⅔ of 100, so search B, not A. B comes back empty: 20 of B's 30 dots go out and 80 are left, so A is 40 of 80 = 50%, B is 10 of 80 = 12½%, C is 25%, D is 12½%. Next: C, although A now holds half the chance: in dots of the first hundred a search of A would still catch ten and a search of C thirteen and a third. Then A. No order of searches is ever ahead of this rule, and the video shows why: each search of a cell puts out a fixed slice of the first hundred dots, a cell's slices only shrink, and the rule takes the biggest slice left (Chew 1967; also checked by trying every order of up to 8 searches). |
| Quizzes | Five, each a row of `model::QUIZZES` with its numbers in `facts.rs` (default for the count): A's chance from the two stories (40%); which cell to search first (B); B's chance after the empty search (12½%); where to search next (C); and how many of nine dots two passes catch, when each fresh pass catches 2 in 3 of what is left (eight: no number of passes makes it certain). Eight more questions are put to the viewer without a ring. |
| The four stories, and what the video says of each | **Palomares, 1966:** where weighing scenarios and keeping the books on how well each area had been searched were first tried; the bomb itself was found by the submersible *Alvin* near where a fisherman had watched it fall. **Scorpion, 1968:** the map of 10,000 simulated submarines; its top cell was E5; the wreck lay in the next cell, F6, within 260 yards of E5's edge; and the uncomfortable part: the map reached the ship two months into the search, and estimates of how wide the sensors saw were several times too generous, so the on-scene numbers had nearly written F6 off. **Air France 447, 2011:** a map from three weighted ingredients, the empty searches folded in, and a second map made because the silence of the recorder beacons was doubted; both maps put their weight near the centre, the search resumed there and found the wreck after about a week, and it lay in a high-chance area of that second map, in water the listening ships had crossed. **The Coast Guard:** the same loop on drifting dots, since 1974 and in its present form since 2007; the 2013 search for John Aldridge, where the first map was wrong because what it had been told was wrong, and new evidence moved it about thirty miles. One table at the end: where each was found against its map. |

| What the script must get right | (1) **The 99.** All were lost; she sank on 22 May 1968 (Navy report NT-24-1); cause undetermined and not speculated on; "as if lives might be saved" is said of the search of 27 May to 5 June, never of the outcome. (2) **Who did what in 1968.** Craven and Andrews named nine scenarios and gave them weights on 18 July; Richardson, of Daniel H. Wagner, Associates, computed the map with 10,000 Monte Carlo runs; Stone and others kept it at sea. The paper does not list the scenarios, so the video does not. (3) **How the weights were set.** The primary source says only that the two men "assigned credences" reflecting, "at least indirectly", the views of operators and specialists. The story that experts bet bottles of whisky is in one journalistic account and nowhere else I could read; it is not told. (4) **Where the wreck was.** In F6 (85 of 10,000), "within 260 yards of the edge of rectangle E5", the top cell (1,250): next to the top cell, not in it. (5) **What the map did not do.** It reached the ship on 12 August; the search had begun on 10 June; the wreck was found on the fifth cruise, when effort went near F6 because of a piece of metal photographed in the summer and because the area had high prior chance. "Bayes found the Scorpion" is not said. (6) **Palomares.** The fisherman's sighting and *Alvin* found the bomb (the official report); the probability work kept the books. (7) **AF 447.** Weights 0.35, 0.35 and 0.30, called subjective by the authors; no sensor credited with more than 0.9; the beacon search at 0.77; "a high probability area" of the alternate map, not "the top cell"; "after one week" of the 2011 search; that the beacons failed is the authors' inference; the search plan was the investigators', informed by the map. (8) **The Coast Guard.** The role of the planner in the Aldridge search is as reported by the New York Times Magazine; the Coast Guard's own releases do not mention it; the helicopter's last leg was drawn by hand through the high-chance area; lives-saved totals are for all cases, nine in ten of which need no search. (9) **The rule.** Rank cells by chance there × chance of seeing, per unit of effort. It is proved best for a thing that stays put, with searches that are fresh tries (independent, with a known chance of seeing) of equal cost (Chew 1967); all three conditions are spoken; it is not claimed for a drifting target. (9a) **The first map.** The update multiplies and never adds, so a place with no dots can never gain one: the method needs a first map that leaves room for the truth, and a new map when evidence says a story was wrong. It is never said that the method does not need the first map to be right. (10) **The chances are knowledge.** The wreck is in one place; the percentages measure what is known, and the authors of the AF 447 analysis say so in as many words. (11) **Koopman.** "Koopman and his colleagues", the US Navy's anti-submarine group, a report printed in 1946; the formula assumes track laid down without regard to what is already swept, and he calls it an estimate "on the conservative side". (12) **Bayes.** An English minister who died in 1761; the rule in the form used here was stated by Laplace. Two sentences at most. (13) **Model numbers** (the model search over the 1968 map, the drift cloud, the 4,000 miles) say "model" on screen. |
