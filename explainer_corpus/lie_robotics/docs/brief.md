| Working title | **Why Robot Arms Think in Lie Groups** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *The Arithmetic of Turning*; *Why a Robot Arm Multiplies*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | Orientations and poses refuse to add, so a robot does its arithmetic on the flat space of velocities that touches the curved space of motions and rolls the answer back on with the exponential: that one move gives the arm's pose as a product of seven screws, its velocity as a sum of them, its inverse kinematics as a few Newton steps, and the straight path between two poses as one screw. |
| The hook (where intuition fails) | Put a book on a table, tilt it a quarter turn about one line on the table and roll it a quarter turn about another; do the same two turns in the other order and the book ends up facing another way. Slides add in any order; turns do not. A robot arm has to put its gripper at a pose (a position and an orientation), so its software must compute with things that refuse to add. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The book quiz itself, as a puzzle with a five-second countdown, answered at once; then the title; then the arm, and the one line the video will earn (`T(q) = e^{[S1]q1} ⋯ e^{[S7]q7} M`), previewed over the gripper corkscrewing down onto a mug. |
| The pictures | **One picture, the screw** (a turn about an axis with a slide along it): a spin held for a second is a screw with no slide; a mobile base on a floor turns about a point; in space every motion is one screw (Chasles, Mozzi); each joint of the arm is one; the gripper's velocity is a sum of them (the Jacobian's columns); the error to a goal is one; the straight path between two poses is one. Under it, **one move**: the space is curved, so do the arithmetic on the flat piece that touches it and roll the answer back on (first shown as a line touching a circle of headings). The opening book returns at the end: how much the order of two turns matters is itself a turn, the Lie bracket. |
| Worked examples | the book: tilt then roll is one turn of 120° about an axis leaning equally from its three edges, roll then tilt another, and the two results differ by 120°; both spins at once give a third answer (127°); headings 170° and −170°: the averaged number points the opposite way (0° against 180°); a spin about the diagonal at 120° a second for one second sends each edge to the next; the KUKA KMR base at 0.5 m/s and 30° a second for 6 s ends 1.91 m to its left, not 3 m ahead; a book moved a quarter turn and (1, 1, 0.5) m is one screw about a vertical axis through (0, 1) rising 0.5 m; **the arm, a KUKA LBR iiwa 14 R820, by hand**: shoulder and elbow a quarter turn each put the gripper's tip at (0.420, 0, 0.886) m, and a quarter turn of joint one swings it to (0, 0.420, 0.886); in that L shape the tip is 0.67 m from the shoulder's axis, 0.42 m from the base's and 0 from joint seven's; with the elbow straight only 5 of 6 directions remain (4 stretched straight out with the wrist straight too, 3 standing straight up); pulling the tip back at 0.1 m/s needs 54°/s of elbow at a 30° bend, 160°/s at 10°, 1,602°/s at 1° (limit 75°/s, met at a bend of 21.5°); the elbow swings on a circle of radius 0.29 m while the gripper holds still; Newton's method reaches a goal half a meter and a quarter turn away in 3 steps (505 mm, 94 mm, 4.2 mm, 0.03 mm); a blend of the two orientations has volume 0.50 at halfway, and the screw path bows 7.6 cm from the straight line; 1,000 steps of adding a spin swell a body by 10.5%; one-degree turns in the two orders differ by 0.0175°. |

| What the script must get right | (1) Turns "usually" do not swap, never "never": turns about one axis swap, and so do two half turns about perpendicular axes (edge test G-X2). (2) "Any turn after any turn is one turn" is Euler's theorem (1775, printed 1776). (3) Gimbal lock is a loss of a direction, not a jam; the Apollo thresholds are the computer's (lamp above 70°, reference dropped above 85°), and the Collins quote is verbatim from the air-to-ground transcript. Lynch and Park never say "gimbal lock"; NASA is the source. (4) "Any three numbers have a bad spot" is said of naming every orientation; the exact statement and its sources are in the fact sheet. (5) Halfway between headings means along the shorter arc. (6) The exponential is the pose reached by holding a velocity for one second; a half turn has two spin arrows. (7) Chasles's theorem includes its edge, a pure slide; Mozzi (1763) before Chasles (1830). (8) The formula is the space form of the product of exponentials; Brockett presented it in 1983 (printed 1984). (9) A singularity is a pose where directions are lost; singular poses also lie inside the reach. The quiz pose has the elbow straight and one direction lost (a slide along the arm). In the L shape the wrist's aligned axes do not cost this seven-joint arm a direction, though they do in some other poses, and the words claim only "a six-joint arm would lose a direction here". (10) "Rarely a tidy formula": this arm does have a closed-form solution; Newton's three steps are for this start and goal, and the method needs a start that is close. (11) A blend of two different turns is never a turn (det = cos²(a/2), tested); re-normalising repairs the shape, not the timing. (11b) The leftover of two turns is a group commutator; the Lie bracket is the rule on the flat piece that it follows for small turns. (11c) Adding two spin arrows is turning about both at once, not one turn after the other. (12) Real planners often use a straight line for the tip instead of the screw; the video says so. (13) Model numbers are labelled "model"; nothing is claimed about how fast KUKA's controller runs. |
