| What the thread says (read in full, nine posts, 23 April 2026) | The owner's four favourites, "4 of my favorite non-AI examples": the cubes 1³ to 5³ cut into slabs that tile a 15 by 15 square; 1/4 + 1/16 + 1/64 + … = 1/3 in a square quartered again and again; (a + b)², (a + b)³ and (a + b)⁴ as coloured blocks; Man-Keung Siu's three stacks of cubes for the sum of squares. His prompt asked for "others that are similarly cool and informative and visually compelling and convincing". He then tried seven machine-proposed ones (odd numbers as L-shaped layers; two triangles of dots; a strip filled by halves; a difference of cubes; (a + b + c)³; Pythagoras by rearrangement, "incredibly famous, so not as impressive"; subsets on a cube) and judged them: "none of these ideas for visual proofs were as good as the sample ones. Ideas are just as important as execution!" His first post also gives the video its honest ending: "they're not really all that rigorous and it's easy to fool yourself into thinking you've proved something when you really haven't, but they're just cool and often give really good intuition for the math." |
| Working title | **Proofs You Can See** (default: the order's working title, kept; alternatives: *Eight Proofs You Can See, and One Picture That Lies*; *Proofs Without Words*). |
| One-sentence takeaway | A proof you can see is one pile of pieces counted two ways: when you can say where every piece goes, and why it would go the same way at every size, the picture is a proof and it shows why the fact is true, which checking cases never does; when a fit is only judged by eye, a picture can lie. |
| The hook (where the obvious approach fails) | Add the cubes: 1, then 1 + 8 = 9, then 36, 100, 225. Every total is a square, of 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Five checks in a row. The obvious approach is to trust the pattern, and it fails honestly on another five-for-five pattern: join points on a circle with straight lines and count the regions: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and then 31 (Leo Moser's note "On the Danger of Induction", 1949). Checking cases never finishes and never says why. A picture can do both. |
| Cold open (≤ 20 s, plan rule 20) | The five cubes fall apart into flat slabs, and the slabs close up into one 15 by 15 square with no gap and nothing left over: the payoff shown as a puzzle ("why would that ever fit?"). Then the title; the pattern and its honest failure open chapter 01. |
| The pictures | Four families, each returning. (1) **The L-shaped layer**: a square grows by one L at a time. With unit squares the layers are the odd numbers (1 + 3 + 5 + … = n²); with slabs cut from cubes they are the cubes (the owner's first picture); and (a + b)² is a square plus one L. (2) **Copies that lock together**: two staircases make a rectangle (1 + 2 + … + n); three stacks of squares make a box (Siu's picture, the owner's fourth). (3) **The same picture at every scale**: a square halved again and again (1/2 + 1/4 + … = 1); a square quartered again and again, where one colour holds one of three equal shares at every scale (the owner's second). (4) **The same pieces, two arrangements**: a cube cut into eight blocks (the owner's third), four triangles in a square (Pythagoras), and four pieces in a triangle that is not a triangle (the picture that lies). The video ends on the rigorous form of its opening picture: the cubes' L-shaped layer for a size nobody drew, with every length named. |
| Proofs, and why these | Eight proofs and one lie. **The owner's four**: the sum of cubes as slabs (two chapters, the centrepiece); the sum of squares as three stacks (two chapters); 1/4 + 1/16 + … = 1/3; (a + b)², ³ and ⁴ as blocks. **Four classics** (default), each chosen because it is exact (every piece counted, no limit of curved shapes), because motion adds what a still cannot, and because it earns one of the owner's four: odd numbers as L-shaped layers (the cubes' picture in its simplest form); two staircases (the side of the cubes' square, and the two-copies idea Siu takes to three); 1/2 + 1/4 + … = 1 (the warm-up for the quartered square); Pythagoras by two arrangements, kept although the owner finds it famous, because it is the honest twin of the lie: the same argument, with the checks done. **The lie**: the missing-square puzzle. Left out, with the reason: the inequality of the arithmetic and geometric means from four rectangles round a square hole (Schattschneider's picture, the only inequality on the list: it stands alone, and the storyboard was a minute over the order's length with it in); the area of a circle by unrolled rings or wedges and Cavalieri's sphere (both are limits of curved pieces, where "what the picture assumed" is the whole proof; they want a video of their own); the difference of cubes from the thread (a good dissection, the same family as the cut cube; no room); (a + b + c)³ and the subsets-on-a-cube picture (the owner found them janky; the count 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 is kept as one line of the blocks chapter); the strip of halves (the square version sets up the quartered square better); the story of young Gauss adding 1 to 100 (the earliest account, from 1856, names no numbers; the 1-to-100 version first appears in 1938). |
| "Shown" and "assumed" | Every proof ends the same way, on a two-line card: **what the picture showed** (the exact statement, with its range) and **what it assumed** (the fit that has to be checked, with the check). The lie fails exactly that card. |
| The next size | A picture is drawn at one size, and five checks prove nothing, so each proof is asked whether the next size would fit, and answers with a picture of the step: one more L-shaped layer; the sixth cube's layer, whose arms are a staircase long; three new slabs under, behind and beside Siu's block (the L-shaped layer in three dimensions, the step his printed picture leaves to the reader); the same square one scale down; a cut that slides. The lie has no such step. |
| The "your turn" device | As Video F's: a card with the question, a visible countdown ring of 4, 5 or 6 seconds by difficulty, the music alone and the narration silent, "pause for more time" on the card every time (spoken at the first quiz), then the answer worked in the same place on screen. | Worked examples | regions for 1 to 6 points: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31; 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25; 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15 = 5 × 6 ÷ 2; 1 + 8 + 27 + 64 + 125 = 225 = 15²: 15 slabs, two of them cut in half, 225 unit squares; the sixth cube's layer: arms 6 wide and 15 long, two and a half slabs each; three stacks of 30 cubes (1 + 4 + 9 + 16) fill a box 4 by 5 by 4½ = 90, and the five-layer block is the four-layer one lifted onto three 5 by 5 slabs; after five halvings 31/32 of the square is filled and the gap is 1/32; after three quarterings each colour holds 21/64 and 1/64 is left; (3 + 2)³ = 27 + 54 + 36 + 8 = 125 in 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 blocks, and the fourth power in 1 + 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 16; legs 3 and 4: 49 = 25 + 4 × 6 = 9 + 16 + 4 × 6; the lie: pieces of 12, 5, 7 and 8 squares make 32, the triangle they seem to fill would hold 32½, the two slopes are 3/8 and 2/5, and the sliver between the two arrangements is never more than 1/8 thick and exactly 1 square in area. The fact sheet has the full list with every quiz. |

| What the script must get right | (1) Five checks are not a proof: the circle's regions need the points placed so that no three lines meet at one inside point, and then 6 points give exactly 31. (2) Nicomachus (about the year 100) wrote that each cube is a run of consecutive odd numbers (1; 3 + 5; 7 + 9 + 11); he did not state the sum-of-cubes rule that carries his name. Aryabhata states it (499); al-Karaji proved it with L-shaped layers around the year 1000. The slab picture with one slab cut in half is Alan Fry's (1985); the three-dimensional drawing in the owner's image is a Wikipedia user's (2015). (3) Even cubes: one slab is cut in two, and the fit of layer k needs the square's side before it to be k(k − 1)/2, which is the staircase result: the proofs depend on each other in the order shown. (4) Siu's box is n by n + 1 by n + ½, and the half comes from one layer sliced flat and turned; the picture is Man-Keung Siu's (Mathematics Magazine, 1984). (5) The infinite sums: after any number of steps the filled part falls short of 1 by exactly the last piece, and no number below 1 is ever enough; "equals 1" is that statement, not "gets close". For the quarters: three colours get equal pieces at every step, and what is left shrinks by four each time. The picture is Sunday Ajose and Roger Nelsen's (1994). Archimedes used the same sum for the parabola, as a finite sum with its remainder; never "Archimedes summed an infinite series". (6) The cut square is Euclid's (Elements II.4, in words, with no letters). The fourth power cannot be drawn as one solid: the picture is two copies of the cut cube, and the count 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 comes from adding neighbours of 1, 3, 3, 1. (7) Pythagoras: both big squares have side a + b, the tilted shape has four equal sides and four right angles (the two sharp angles of a right triangle make a right angle), and the triangles meet along straight edges. Nobody knows who first drew it; never "Pythagoras's own proof", never "a Chinese proof from 200 BC" (the Chinese picture is a different one, from a third-century commentary). (8) The lie: Paul Curry, a New York amateur magician, had the idea in 1953; Martin Gardner published it in 1956 and found the triangle form himself. Never "Curry's triangle was published in 1953". (9) What turns a picture into a proof: every piece accounted for, every fit given a reason, and the reason the same at every size. A program's check up to some size is still a check, and the video says so about its own. |
